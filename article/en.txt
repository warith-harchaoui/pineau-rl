META Research | 2026-11-15 | November 15, 2026 | Reinforcement Learning: Did we listen to Joelle Pineau in 2018? | In 2018, Joelle Pineau projected two well-separated curves that came out of the same program. Eight years later, we reread the papers from 2018 to 2026 and reran her experiments with the algorithms of 2026. Four practices changed, others did not move. Her most famous number finally finds its explanation.

BRIEF In short | Contents

Reinforcement learning (RL) teaches a machine to act by trial and error, guided by a numerical reward. In 2018, Joelle Pineau showed two major conferences why the published results of this field inspired little trust. We took up her list of sixteen flaws with two instruments: a rereading of papers from 2018 to 2026 with her grid, then her experiments rerun under a protocol written in advance.

- **What changed.** With the same grid, in classical RL, linking the code went from 31% to 74% of papers, quantifying the compute from 27% to 58%, showing the effect of a setting from 44% to 80%, reporting an uncertainty from 63% to 88%.
- **What shows no detectable change.** The statistical test (6% then 12%), baselines described with all their settings, evaluation on held-out conditions.
- **What our experiments add.** Two groups of runs drawn at random rarely fool a statistical test; two sorted groups fool it nearly one time in three. Keeping the best three runs out of ten inflates the mean by a third. The t of 9 from 2018 comes from a test that counts episodes instead of random seeds.

ENDBRIEF

## two-curves | Two curves, one program
In spring 2018, in Vancouver, Joelle Pineau takes the stage at ICLR, one of the three major conferences of machine learning[[1]]. She is a professor at McGill University and heads Facebook's artificial intelligence research lab in Montreal. In December, in Montreal, she gives an expanded version of the talk at NeurIPS, the largest of the three[[2]].

She projects two learning curves. An articulated cheetah, drawn in two dimensions in a physics simulator, learns to run. Each curve follows its score over training, averaged over five runs. The two curves separate clearly. A statistical test declares them different: t = −9.09, p = 0.0016. Any reviewer would see two algorithms, one better than the other.

It is a single program, TRPO, with the same settings. Only the random seed changes, the starting number from which the computer draws all the chance of a training run. Pineau points out on stage that the two groups are not sorted: they are "just five runs" on each side. Chance alone has manufactured a publishable "improvement."

Two average-return curves on HalfCheetah, each the mean of five runs: the orange one ends clearly above the blue one. | Two groups of five training runs of the same TRPO program on HalfCheetah, differing only in their random seed. Joelle Pineau, NeurIPS 2018, [page 22 of her slides](https://media.neurips.cc/Conferences/NIPS2018/Slides/jpineau-NeurIPS-dec18-fb.pdf), after Henderson and coauthors (2018).

The figure comes from a paper by her team, signed by Peter Henderson and his coauthors[[3]]. It serves a broader point. Pineau starts from a survey by the journal *Nature*: of 1,576 researchers from every discipline, more than 70% said they had already failed to reproduce a colleague's experiment[[4]]. She then quotes two Stanford researchers, Buckheit and Donoho, who wrote in 1995 that a scientific paper about computation is merely the advertising of the research. The research itself is the complete software and the instructions that produced the figures[[5]]. Then she shows, figure after figure, what her field lacks to deserve trust.

## the-cheetah-the-seed | The cheetah, the random seed and the test
A few words are enough to follow what comes next. A program, the agent, acts in a simulated world. At each step it observes a situation, chooses an action and receives a numerical reward. It looks for the way of acting that earns the most in total. Our running example is called HalfCheetah, the cheetah on the slide: the agent drives its six joints and scores points when the cheetah moves forward. At first it wriggles. After a million steps it runs.

Training draws many numbers at random: the starting weights of the neural network, the exploration actions, the cheetah's initial position. Same program, same random seed: same result. Same program, another random seed: another result. A "run" here means one complete training with a given random seed.

The settings the experimenter fixes by hand, such as the learning speed or the size of the network, are called hyperparameters. To show that a new method does better, a paper compares it with existing methods, the baselines. The comparison is fair when all of them received the same care in tuning.

That leaves the referee. Two averages always differ a little. A statistical test puts a number on the probability of seeing a gap at least as large if the two methods were in truth equivalent: this is the p-value. Below 0.05, custom declares the gap "significant."

## sixteen-flaws | Sixteen flaws in four families
Pineau does not settle for a general reproach. She shows each flaw through an example. We counted sixteen in her two talks, sorted into four families.

- **The material.** The paper appears without its code, without the exact settings or the compute needed, without the versions of the software that are part of the result.
- **The tuning.** Hyperparameters are given in part, the way they were chosen goes unsaid. Baselines receive less care than the method being defended.
- **Random seeds and statistics.** Three or five runs, never ten in the table she shows. Only the best ones published. Two groups of the same code that look like two algorithms. No test.
- **The reach of the conclusions.** The best algorithm changes from one task to the next, two implementations of the same algorithm diverge, the agent is evaluated in the very world it trained in, on benchmarks so simple it learns them by heart.

Eight years have passed. NeurIPS adopted the checklist Pineau proposed in 2019[[6]]. Did we listen to her? Two instruments answer. The first rereads papers with her reading grid, extended for the flaws she left unmeasured. The second reruns her experiments with the algorithms of 2026, under a protocol written before the first training run, where any later change is logged with its date and its reason. These experiments are being consolidated, with twenty random seeds instead of ten, five instead of three for the language model part and several strengths of modified physics: the numbers that follow are those of ten random seeds, as our document gives them today.

## same-grid | Reread 2018 before judging 2026
Pineau had read 50 RL papers from 2018 and scored eight criteria for each. We took every paper from ICLR 2026, ICML 2026 and NeurIPS 2025 whose title mentions reinforcement learning: 1,252 out of 17,836. A first random draw kept 50. Among them, 27 apply RL to large language models, 14 belong to classical RL (robotics, games, control) and 9 are theoretical. Fourteen papers are too few to detect a gap of thirty points, so we topped classical RL up to 50.

The readers are language model agents, which read the whole PDF, appendices included. Each decision comes with an exact quotation from the paper and its page, which anyone can check in the repository. Reread by a second agent that did not see the first reading, ten papers give the same verdict on 127 decisions out of 130 (98%). No human reread the papers blind.

The first surprise: our reading of 2018 departs from Pineau's figures. We reread 50 papers from 2018 ourselves with the same grid. Our reading finds 55% of papers giving all the settings of their method, where Pineau counted 90%. Comparing our 2026 papers with her figures would mix two changes, that of the practices and that of the grid. Each verdict therefore compares the classical RL of 2026 with our own reading of 2018, with the same grids and the same readers.

Four practices changed. Linking the code went from 31% to 74% of papers, showing the effect of a setting from 44% to 80%, quantifying the compute from 27% to 58%, reporting an uncertainty (error bars or a band around the curve) from 63% to 88%. These rises withstand every cut we tried.

Other practices show no detectable change. The statistical test goes from 6% to 12%, baselines described with all their settings from 35% to 29%, evaluation on held-out conditions from 33% to 45%, the version of the benchmarks from 23% to 26%. With 50 papers on each side, a change of ten points still slips by unnoticed. Papers draw bars and rarely test.

## a-slow-climb | A slow climb that began before the checklist
A before and an after on two points do not say when the change happened. So we read 20 classical RL papers per year from ICML and NeurIPS, in 2019, 2020, 2022 and 2024, drawn at random and read with both grids.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-trajectoire-en.svg | Five curves from 2018 to 2026. Uncertainty from 63% to 88%, with 100% in 2024. Code linked from 31% to 74%. Effect of a setting from 44% to 80%. Compute stated from 27% to 58%. Statistical test from 6% to 12%, with 25% in 2024. | Share of classical RL papers that follow each practice, year by year: 48 papers from 2018, 20 per year from 2019 to 2024, 50 from 2026. With 20 papers, a share has an uncertainty of about twenty points either way. The four upper practices climb steadily. The statistical test, in grey, shows no trend.

The four practices climb steadily, with no clear jump right after the 2019 checklist. Only the effect of a setting rises clearly later, from 40% over 2019 and 2020 to 73% over 2022 and 2024. These series describe a slow rise that began before the checklist or with it. They do not say what caused it.

The checklist itself holds a surprise. Every NeurIPS paper attaches the checklist filled in by its authors. Of the 30 NeurIPS 2025 papers we had read, the question on statistical significance settles for error bars "or other appropriate information." The 18 papers that answer yes apply no test. On code, 5 papers out of 22 answer yes with no link to their code. A ticked box does not guarantee the practice it names.

No simple rule sorts what moved. Showing the effect of a setting takes extra training runs: the practice progressed. Giving the version of a benchmark takes one line of text: it did not change.

## thirty-seeds | Thirty random seeds, one program
On to the most famous flaw. We redid the 2018 experiment on a larger scale: thirty training runs of PPO, the most widely used algorithm, on HalfCheetah, a million steps each, with the same code and the same hyperparameters. Only the random seed changes.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-e1-graines-en.svg | Swarm of thirty dots on a score axis. Twenty-seven dots between 1,138 and 1,999, three isolated dots beyond 3,000. | Thirty training runs of the same program on the same task, PPO on the simulated cheetah. Each dot is one run, placed at its final score. Only the random seed changes from one dot to the next. Mean 1,688, median 1,580.

The mean is 1,688, the standard deviation 568, or 34% of the mean. Twenty-seven runs end between 1,138 and 1,999; three take off beyond 3,000. Nothing like a bell curve, although no line of the program changes from one run to the next.

We then drew two groups from these thirty runs 10,000 times and applied Welch's test to each pair, a version of the t-test that does not assume the two spreads are equal. Both groups come from the same program: every gap declared significant is an error of the test, a false positive.

FIG std | rl-pineau-e1-faux-positifs-en.svg | Bar chart. Five best against five worst out of ten runs: 31% false positives. Ten against ten at random: 1.7%. Three against three at random: 1.6%. Five against five at random: 1.2%. | Share of the 10,000 draws in which the test declares two groups of runs of the same program different, at the usual 5% threshold. Drawn at random, the groups fool the test less often than that threshold. Sorted, they fool it nearly one time in three.

A surprise: two groups of five drawn at random almost never fool the test, in 1.2% of the draws. They do fool the eye. Their means differ by 17% at the median and by 47% one time in twenty, enough to draw two well-separated curves. The test gives way when the runs are sorted: setting the five best runs against the five worst gives 31% false positives. Chance fools the eye; sorting fools the test.

## the-t-of-nine | The riddle of the t of 9
One mystery remained. If groups drawn at random fool the test so rarely, where does the t of 9 from 2018 come from? Henderson and his coauthors did not test final scores. Their caption speaks of a t-test averaged over the whole learning curve. We redid that computation on our thirty curves. One time in twenty, the absolute value of the mean t exceeds 1.4 when the groups are drawn at random, 2.5 when they are sorted. None of our 20,000 draws reaches 9.

First lead: their algorithm, TRPO, perhaps spreads more than PPO. We trained TRPO thirty times, with a plan written before any training. It spreads far more: its standard deviation is 48% of its mean. Yet the mean t exceeds 1.4 only one time in twenty at random, 1.9 when sorted. Another reading stacks the hundred points of each group's five curves as if they were independent. It climbs to 5.0 at random and 6.3 sorted. Worse, it declares a difference in 53% of the random draws, between two groups of the same code. No reading reaches 9.

The published number held the clue itself. A t of 9.09 with p = 0.0016 implies about 3.4 degrees of freedom, the number that sets how strict the test is. In June 2017, Peter Henderson had published a script with a telling name, "from the same distribution"[[7]]. It averages the curves of the five random seeds in each group. Then, at each iteration, it compares the two groups with a test built on the spread between episodes, the successive rounds that one agent plays during training. The spread between random seeds, the one the figure was meant to show, never enters the test.

We applied this script to our thirty TRPO runs, with a plan written before the computation. The mean t exceeds 9.09 in 38% of the random draws. The test declares a difference in 43% of the cases. One number gives the reason: in our TRPO, the standard deviation between the episodes of a single agent is 7% of the standard deviation between agents. With PPO, whose episodes vary more, the same computation never reaches 9.

FIG std | rl-pineau-t-de-neuf-en.svg | Bar chart. TRPO with the 2017 script: 38% of draws reach 9.09. PPO with our computation, TRPO with our computation, TRPO with stacked points, PPO with the 2017 script: 0%. | Two groups of five runs drawn at random from thirty, 10,000 times, for each algorithm and each way of computing t. The bar gives the share of draws in which the absolute value of the mean t reaches 9.09, the number published in 2018. Only the 2017 script, applied to TRPO, gets there.

Nothing proves that this script produced the 2018 figure. Its name and its output make it likely. The t of 9 required no sorting: it comes from a test that counts episodes instead of random seeds. The flaw runs deeper than the figure said. The chance of the random seeds fooled the very test that was meant to measure it.

## the-best-runs | Keep the best, inflate by a third
Pineau showed another practice: launch ten runs and publish the mean of the best ones. Every number is true, yet the one published exceeds what the method gives on average. On our thirty PPO runs, keeping the best three out of ten inflates the mean by 33%: 2,249 instead of 1,692.

We applied the same selection to four algorithms on four tasks, ten random seeds each. The algorithms are PPO, written in two libraries (PyTorch and JAX), then SAC and CrossQ, a descendant of SAC published in 2024. The tasks are the cheetah, a one-legged hopper (Hopper), a two-legged walker (Walker2d) and a swimmer (Swimmer).

FIG wide | rl-pineau-meilleurs-sur-dix-en.svg | Bar chart grouped by task, four bars per task. HalfCheetah: SAC 19%, CrossQ 74%, PPO PyTorch 35%, PPO JAX 47%. Hopper: 78, 49, 29, 20. Walker2d: 63, 9, 58, 84. Swimmer: 8, 8, 18, 6. | Rise of the mean when only the best three of the ten runs are published, for each algorithm and each task. All sixteen rises are positive: sorting always serves whoever does it.

The mean inflates by 32% at the median, from 6% to 84% depending on the algorithm and the task. Of 24 comparisons between two algorithms, 3 flip. Two of them pit the two versions of PPO against each other: when the gap is small, keeping one's best runs is enough to pick the winner.

In papers, the practice rarely shows: 14% of classical RL papers from 2026 let it show, against 19% in 2018. A paper that keeps its best runs without saying so escapes our reading, so these shares are minimums. The number of runs, for its part, has not changed. Of 41 experimental RL papers in the first 2026 draw, 20 do not say how many training runs support their results, 4 have only one and the median among the others is five, the most frequent value in Pineau's table. Yet between two of our algorithms whose means differ by less than 20%, five random seeds recover the right order only 71 times out of a hundred at the median. Such a gap reads backwards nearly one time in three.

## the-leader-changes | The leader changes with the task
In 2018, Henderson and his coauthors showed that the algorithm leading on the cheetah stopped leading on the hopper or the swimmer. Our four algorithms of 2026 do the same. Each score is an interquartile mean: drop the lowest quarter and the highest quarter of the ten runs, then average the rest[[8]].

FIG wide | rl-pineau-classement-taches-en.svg | Bar chart grouped by task, score as a percentage of the best. HalfCheetah: SAC 100, CrossQ 92, PPO PyTorch 27, PPO JAX 24. Hopper: SAC 43, CrossQ 75, PPO PyTorch 94, PPO JAX 100. Walker2d: SAC 23, CrossQ 100, PPO PyTorch 40, PPO JAX 23. Swimmer: SAC 42, CrossQ 98, PPO PyTorch 96, PPO JAX 100. | Each algorithm's score relative to the best on its task, over ten random seeds: the bar at 100 marks the leader. PPO trains for a million steps, SAC and CrossQ for 200,000.

SAC leads on the cheetah, PPO written in JAX on the hopper and the swimmer, CrossQ on the walker. SAC, first on the cheetah, is clearly beaten on the hopper and on the walker. Yet the leader clearly beats all the others only on the walker: on the other three tasks, ten random seeds do not separate it from the runner-up.

The two versions of PPO teach the same lesson. On the walker, PPO written in PyTorch scores 2,158, PPO written in JAX 1,272, or 41% less. The two intervals still overlap with ten random seeds. A method compared with "PPO" on this task would win or lose depending on the version chosen, without ten random seeds being able to settle it.

## modified-physics | Twenty percent heavier
Pineau asked for training and evaluation to be kept apart. A student who saw the exam paper the day before passes without having understood anything. In RL, the agent is often evaluated in the very world it trained in. We evaluated our agents twice, with random seeds never seen before: in the physics of their training, then with masses 20% heavier and friction 20% lower.

FIG std | rl-pineau-physique-modifiee-en.svg | Rank chart on Hopper, two columns. Training physics: PPO JAX first, PPO PyTorch second, CrossQ third, SAC fourth. Modified physics: CrossQ first with 1,302, SAC second with 751, PPO PyTorch third with 609, PPO JAX last with 525. | The one-legged hopper, four algorithms, ten random seeds each. On the left, the rank in the training physics; on the right, the rank with heavier masses and lower friction, with the score reached. The ranks compare interquartile means, without a test.

Twelve pairs of algorithm and task out of sixteen lose a detectable share of their score. The loss depends on the algorithm. On the hopper, PPO keeps only 22% of its score in its PyTorch version and 18% in its JAX version, while SAC keeps 64% and CrossQ 65%. On the swimmer, none loses; SAC even gains 7%. PPO written in JAX, first in the hopper's training physics, ends last there. CrossQ takes the lead. On the walker, SAC moves from last place to second.

A ranking set in the training physics says little about robustness. Have papers changed on this point? In classical RL, evaluation on held-out conditions goes from 33% to 45% of papers, with no detectable change. It has become the rule elsewhere, in the RL of large language models, whose problem collections separate training and test by construction.

## a-baseline-too-good | A baseline too good to be true
The grid applies to oneself first. One of our experiments set out to illustrate the badly tuned baseline. It sets PPO on the hopper with its library's default values against PPO with the settings published by the RL Baselines3 Zoo collection, the reference for that library[[9]]. First result: 2,877 for the default values, 1,003 for the tuned version. Would the published settings do three times worse than the defaults?

The result was too good. Checking, we found an error on our side: our code normalised the observations with a discount factor of 0.99, where the collection uses 0.999. The corrected recipe scores 1,012 on version 5 of the task and 984 on version 4, the one the collection was tuned for. Our error was real, yet it explained nothing. Ten agents trained with the collection's official script score 995, against 2,423 for the default values. The published recipe has aged with the software around it. A result that looks too good gets checked all the way.

## coin-flip | A coin-flip reward
Today RL serves mostly to fine-tune language models. They are given a math problem, a program checks the answer, the right ones are rewarded. In 2025, a team at the University of Washington showed that a reward drawn at random improves Qwen models in math, but not Llama or OLMo models[[10]]. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B and the MATH-500 benchmark, the true reward gains 29.1 percentage points, a reward drawn at random 21.4.

We wanted to see the phenomenon up close, on a small scale. We wrote GRPO ourselves, one of the most widely used algorithms for this purpose[[11]], then applied it to two small open models: Qwen2.5 with 0.5 billion parameters and Llama 3.2 with 1 billion. Each model trains for a hundred steps with the true reward, then with a coin-flip reward, three random seeds each time.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-grpo-graines-en.svg | Grouped bar chart. Qwen with the true reward: 36%, 61% and 13% depending on the random seed. Qwen with a coin flip: 1%, 0%, 0%. Llama with the true reward: 20%, 48%, 25%. Llama with a coin flip: 0%, 1%, 0%. | Share of problems solved at the first attempt after a hundred training steps, for each model, each reward and each random seed. With the true reward, the same program gives very different results from one random seed to the next. With a coin flip, success drops to zero or nearly so.

On Qwen, with the true reward, success at the first attempt starts at about 10% and ends at 36%, 61% and 13% depending on the random seed: the 2018 flaw in miniature. The run at 13% ran on the processor after a crash, so its random seed and the hardware are mixed up in it. On Llama, it starts at 23% to 25% and ends at 20%, 48% and 25%. The mean gain is +26 points on Qwen, with an interval from +2 to +51, against +7 points on Llama, from −4 to +22. With three random seeds, these intervals stay coarse. Llama's contains zero: nothing allows us to say that GRPO improves Llama here. With the reward drawn by chance, success falls to 1% or less in all six runs. Our part remains an illustration on small models, far from a replication of the Washington team's work.

## the-cutoff | The cutoff trap
This part brought a benchmark trap to light. The model's answers are cut at 160 tokens, the pieces of words it produces one by one. With the base model, 23 answers out of 32 hit that limit before giving a result. None of these cut answers is right.

FIG std | rl-pineau-grpo-coupure-en.svg | Stacked bar chart, four multiplication problems, eight answers each. 48 × 49: 1 right, 7 cut. 14 × 36: 2 right, 1 wrong, 5 cut. 21 × 50: 1 right, 2 wrong, 5 cut. 45 × 17: 1 right, 1 wrong, 6 cut. | The answers of the Qwen2.5 model with 0.5 billion parameters before any training, eight per problem. Most hit the 160-token limit before giving a result and count as wrong.

With the true reward, the model first learns to finish before the cutoff, which the reward drawn by chance does not teach. On Qwen, the share of cut answers ends between 59% and 98% with a coin flip, between 0% and 29% with the true reward. Part of the measured "gain" comes from a detail of the benchmark. We now measure the share of cut answers at every evaluation.

## what-we-keep | Listened to in part
Joelle Pineau was listened to in part. With the same grid, four practices progressed in classical RL: linking the code, quantifying the compute, showing the effect of a setting, uncertainty bars. The statistical test, baselines tuned and described with the same care as the method, held-out evaluation and stated versions show no detectable change.

Our experiments sharpen the picture of the most famous flaw. On our runs, random seeds manufacture visible gaps. Our choices, sorting and keeping the best, turn those gaps into false positives. The t of 9 comes from another choice, a test that counts episodes. We draw three rules from this:

- never sort random seeds before comparing;
- show an interval next to every curve;
- test on random seeds, never on episodes.

We found an error in our own code, then saw that it explained nothing. Our first indicator on language models measured a length limit. Both traps surfaced because the protocol was written in advance and because we checked surprising results before publishing them. That was Pineau's advice in 2018: be as critical of one's good results as of one's bad ones.

## seven-questions | Seven questions for reading an RL claim
The checklist is addressed to authors. The reader of a paper, a press release or a demo draws seven questions from it, none of which requires knowing how to program.

- **How many random seeds?** With fewer than five runs, or with "the best runs," the result says little.
- **What uncertainty?** An average with no interval and no band around the curve gives no way to judge a gap.
- **Which baseline, tuned how?** If the method received a month of tuning and the baseline its default values, the gap measures the month of tuning.
- **How many tasks, how many model families?** A result obtained on a single task with a single starting model holds for that pair.
- **Evaluated where?** An agent judged on the situations of its training may have learned them by heart.
- **Which code?** Without published code, the reader does not know what the paper really did.
- **What compute?** A result whose compute nobody else pays for is not reproduced.
