META Research | 2026-11-24 | November 24, 2026 | Reinforcement Learning: Did we listen to Joelle Pineau in 2018? | In 2018, Joelle Pineau projected two well-separated curves that came out of the same program. Eight years later, we reread the papers from 2018 to 2026 and reran her experiments with the algorithms of 2026. Four practices changed, others did not move. Her most famous number finally finds its explanation.

BRIEF In short | Contents

Reinforcement learning (RL) teaches a machine to act by trial and error, guided by a numerical reward. In 2018, Joelle Pineau showed two major conferences why the published results of this field inspired little trust. We took up her list of sixteen flaws with two instruments: a rereading of papers from 2018 to 2026 with her grid, then her experiments rerun under a protocol written in advance.

- **What changed.** With the same grid, in classical RL, linking the code went from 31% to 74% of papers, quantifying the compute from 27% to 58%, showing the effect of a setting from 44% to 80%, reporting an uncertainty from 63% to 88%. This last rise is the most fragile: it does not withstand all our checks.
- **What shows no detectable change.** The statistical test (6% then 12%), baselines described with all their settings, evaluation on held-out conditions.
- **What our experiments add.** Two groups of runs drawn at random rarely fool a statistical test. Two sorted groups fool it nearly one time in three. Keeping the best three runs out of ten inflates the mean by a third. The t of 9 from 2018 comes from a test that counts episodes instead of random seeds.

ENDBRIEF

## two-curves | Two curves, one program
In the spring of 2018, in Vancouver, Joelle Pineau walks onto the stage of ICLR, one of the three major conferences of machine learning[[1]]. She is a professor at McGill University and runs Facebook's artificial intelligence research lab in Montreal. In December, in Montreal, she takes up and extends her talk before NeurIPS, the largest of the three[[2]].

VIDEO Vh4H0gOwdIg | Joelle Pineau, ICLR 2018: Reproducibility, Reusability, and Robustness in Deep Reinforcement Learning | The Vancouver talk, in full.

She shows two learning curves. An articulated cheetah, drawn in two dimensions inside a physics simulator, learns to run. Each curve follows its score over training, averaged over five runs. The two curves pull clearly apart. A statistical test declares them different: t = −9.09, p = 0.0016. Any reviewer would see two algorithms, one better than the other.

It is a single program, TRPO, with the same settings. Only the random seed changes, the starting number from which the computer draws all the chance in a training run. Pineau says on stage that the two groups are not sorted: they are "just five runs" on each side. Chance alone produced a publishable "improvement".

The figure comes from a paper by her team, signed by Peter Henderson and his co-authors[[3]]. It serves a broader point. Pineau starts from a survey by the journal *Nature*: of 1,576 researchers from every discipline, more than 70% said they had already failed to reproduce a colleague's experiment[[4]]. She then quotes two Stanford researchers, Buckheit and Donoho, who wrote in 1995 that a scientific paper about computation is merely the advertising of the research. The research itself is the complete software and the instructions that produced the figures[[5]]. Then she shows, figure after figure, what her field lacks to deserve trust.

## the-cheetah-the-seed | The cheetah, the random seed and the test
A few words are enough to follow the rest. A program, the agent, acts in a simulated world. At each step, it observes a situation, chooses an action and receives a numerical reward. It looks for the way of acting that pays the most in total. The cheetah of the slide is called HalfCheetah: the agent drives its six joints and scores points when the cheetah moves forward. At first it wriggles, after a million steps it runs. Three other simulated bodies appear later: Hopper, a one-legged hopper, Walker2d, a two-legged walker, and Swimmer, a swimmer.

Training draws many numbers at random: the starting weights of the neural network, the exploration actions, the initial position of the cheetah. Same program, same random seed: same result. Same program, another random seed: another result. A "run" here means a complete training with a given random seed.

The settings the experimenter fixes by hand, such as the learning rate or the size of the network, are called hyperparameters. To show that a new method does better, a paper compares it with existing methods, the baselines. The comparison is fair when all of them received the same care in tuning.

Two means always differ a little, even between two identical methods. A statistical test says whether the observed gap exceeds what chance usually produces. To do so it computes a number, the t: the larger the t, the sturdier the gap looks. From it comes the p-value, the probability of seeing a gap at least this large if the two methods were in truth equivalent. Below 0.05, one chance in twenty, custom calls the gap "significant". A 95% interval gives the range where the true value very probably lies: if it contains zero, the gap may be nothing but chance.

## sixteen-flaws | Sixteen flaws in four families
Pineau shows each flaw with an example. We found sixteen in her two talks, sorted into four families.

- **The material.** The paper comes out without its code, without the exact settings or the compute required, without the software versions that are part of the result.
- **The tuning.** Hyperparameters are given in part, how they were chosen goes unsaid. Baselines get less care than the method being defended.
- **Random seeds and statistics.** Three or five runs, never ten in the table she shows. Only the best published. Two groups of the same code that look like two algorithms. No test.
- **The reach of the conclusions.** The best algorithm changes from one task to the next, two implementations of the same algorithm diverge, the agent is evaluated in the very world it trained in, on benchmarks, the standard tasks everyone compares on, so simple it learns them by heart.

Eight years have passed. NeurIPS adopted the checklist Pineau proposed in 2019[[6]]. Did we listen to her? Two instruments answer. The first rereads papers with her reading grid, extended for the flaws it left unmeasured. The second reruns her experiments with the algorithms of 2026, under a protocol written before the first training run, where every later change is logged with its date and reason. We then reran these experiments with twenty random seeds instead of ten. The protocol had fixed in advance that the first ten are authoritative: the numbers below are theirs. We say where the twenty qualify them.

## same-grid | Reread 2018 before judging 2026
Pineau had read 50 RL papers from 2018 and scored eight criteria for each. We took every paper of ICLR 2026, ICML 2026 and NeurIPS 2025 whose title mentions reinforcement learning: 1,252 out of 17,836. A first random draw kept 50. Among them, 27 apply RL to large language models, 14 belong to classical RL (robotics, games, control) and 9 are theoretical. Fourteen papers are too few to detect a gap of thirty points: we topped classical RL up to 50.

The readers are artificial intelligence programs, agents built on a language model like the one behind ChatGPT. They read the whole PDF, appendices included. Each decision comes with an exact quotation from the paper and its page, which anyone can check in the repository. Reread by a second agent blind to the first reading, ten papers give the same verdict on 127 decisions out of 130 (98%). All these readers are copies of the same language model: this agreement measures its consistency, not a human's judgement. No human reread the papers blind. A "no" could also hide a reader's oversight. So, with keywords fixed in advance, we searched for whatever could contradict each "no". Of 377 "no" decisions, 10 turn into "yes", 2 in 2018 and 8 in 2026. No verdict changes.

A first gap, with Pineau herself. We reread 50 papers from 2018 with her grid. Our reading finds 55% of papers giving all the settings of their method, where Pineau counted 90%. Comparing our 2026 papers with her numbers would mix two changes, that of practice and that of the reading. Each verdict therefore compares classical RL in 2026 with our own reading of 2018, with the same grids and the same readers.

Four practices changed. Linking the code went from 31% to 74% of papers, showing the effect of a setting from 44% to 80%, quantifying the compute from 27% to 58%, reporting an uncertainty (error bars or a band around the curve) from 63% to 88%. Comparing eight practices gives chance eight opportunities to produce a false gap, so we made the threshold stricter, as is customary in that case. Code and compute pass it under every cut we tried, the effect of a setting under almost all. The rise in uncertainty is fragile: two of our checks make it fail, with p = 0.07 each time, above the threshold.

The other practices show no detectable change. The statistical test goes from 6% to 12%, baselines described with all their settings from 35% to 29%, evaluation on held-out conditions from 33% to 45%, the benchmark version from 23% to 26%. With 50 papers on each side, a change of ten points still goes unnoticed. Papers draw error bars, they rarely test.

## a-slow-climb | A slow climb that began before the checklist
A before and an after on two points do not say when the change happened. So we read 20 classical RL papers per year from ICML and NeurIPS, in 2019, 2020, 2022 and 2024, drawn at random and read with both grids.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-trajectoire-en.svg | Five lines from 2018 to 2026, a dotted vertical line at 2019 for the NeurIPS checklist. Uncertainty from 63% to 88%, with 100% in 2024. Code linked from 31% to 74%. Effect of a setting from 44% to 80%. Compute stated from 27% to 58%. Statistical test, in grey, from 6% to 12%, with 25% in 2024. | Share of classical RL papers following each practice, year by year: 48 papers from 2018, 20 per year from 2019 to 2024, 50 from 2026. With 20 papers, a share carries an uncertainty of about twenty points either way. The four upper practices climb steadily. The statistical test, in grey, shows no trend.

The four practices climb steadily, with no clear jump right after the 2019 checklist. Only the effect of a setting rises clearly later, from 40% over 2019 and 2020 to 73% over 2022 and 2024. These series describe a slow rise that began before the checklist or with it. They do not say what caused it.

The checklist itself holds a discovery. Every NeurIPS paper attaches the checklist filled in by its authors. Of the 30 NeurIPS 2025 papers we had read, the question on statistical significance settles for error bars "or other appropriate information". The 18 papers that answer yes apply no test. On code, 5 papers out of 22 answer yes without a link to their code. A ticked box does not guarantee the practice it names.

What moved is not always the easiest. Showing the effect of a setting takes extra training runs: the practice progressed all the same. Giving a benchmark's version takes a single line of text: it did not change.

## thirty-seeds | Thirty random seeds, one program
Now to the most famous flaw. We redid the 2018 experiment on a larger scale: thirty training runs of PPO, the most widely used algorithm, on HalfCheetah, a million steps each, with the same code and the same hyperparameters. Only the random seed changes.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-e1-graines-en.svg | Thirty dots on a score axis, stacked where they crowd. Twenty-seven dots between 1,138 and 1,999, three red dots isolated beyond 3,000, up to 3,430. A dotted line marks the median, 1,580. | Thirty training runs of the same program on the same task, PPO on HalfCheetah. Each dot is a run, placed at its final score. Only the random seed changes from one dot to the next. Mean 1,688, median 1,580.

The mean is 1,688. The standard deviation, which says how far a run usually strays from the mean, is 568, or 34% of the mean. Twenty-seven runs end between 1,138 and 1,999, three take off beyond 3,000. Nothing like a bell curve, although not one line of the program changes from one run to the next.

We then drew two groups from these thirty runs 10,000 times, then applied Welch's test to each pair, the most common test for comparing two means. Both groups come from the same program: every gap declared significant is an error of the test, a false positive.

FIG std | rl-pineau-e1-faux-positifs-en.svg | Horizontal bars, a dotted line at 5%. Three against three at random: 1.6%. Five against five at random: 1.2%. Ten against ten at random: 1.7%. Five best against five worst out of ten runs, in red: 31%. | Share of the 10,000 draws where the test declares two groups of runs of the same program different, at the usual 5% threshold. Drawn at random, the groups fool the test less often than this threshold. Sorted, they fool it nearly one time in three.

The result defies intuition. Two groups of five drawn at random almost never fool the test, in 1.2% of the draws. They fool the eye, though. Their means differ by 17% at the median and by 47% one time in twenty, enough to draw two well-separated curves. The test gives way when we sort: setting the five best runs against the five worst gives 31% false positives. Chance fools the eye, sorting fools the test.

## the-t-of-nine | The riddle of the t of 9
Where, then, does the t of 9 from 2018 come from, if groups drawn at random so rarely fool the test? Henderson and his co-authors did not test final scores. Their caption speaks of an average t over the whole learning curve: the test is rerun at each point of the curve, then the t values are averaged. We redid this computation on our thirty curves. One time in twenty, this average t exceeds 1.4 when the groups are drawn at random, 2.5 when they are sorted. None of our 20,000 draws reaches 9.

First lead: their algorithm, TRPO, may spread more than PPO. We trained TRPO thirty times, with a plan written before any training. It spreads far more, its standard deviation is 48% of its mean. Yet the average t exceeds 1.4 only one time in twenty at random, 1.9 sorted. Another reading stacks the hundred points of each group's five curves as if they were independent. It climbs to 5.0 at random and 6.3 sorted. Worse, it declares a difference in 53% of the random draws, between two groups of the same code. No reading reaches 9.

The published number held its own clue. A t of 9.09 with p = 0.0016 implies about 3.4 degrees of freedom, a setting of the test that depends on how many measurements are compared. This number gives away the computation used. In June 2017, Peter Henderson had published a script with a telling name, "from the same distribution"[[7]]. It averages the curves of the five random seeds of each group. Then, at each iteration, it compares the two groups with a test built on the spread between episodes, the successive games one agent plays during training. The spread between random seeds, the one the figure was meant to show, never enters the test.

We applied this script to our thirty TRPO runs, with a plan written before the computation. The average t exceeds 9.09 in 38% of random draws. The test declares a difference in 43% of the cases. One number gives the reason: in our TRPO, the standard deviation between episodes of the same agent is 7% of the standard deviation between agents. With PPO, whose episodes vary more, the same computation never reaches 9.

FIG std | rl-pineau-t-de-neuf-en.svg | Horizontal bars. TRPO with the 2017 script, in red: 38% of draws reach 9.09. PPO with our computation, TRPO with our computation, TRPO with stacked points, PPO with the 2017 script: 0%, a plain grey dot. | Two groups of five runs drawn at random from thirty, 10,000 times, for each algorithm and each way of computing t. The bar gives the share of draws where the absolute value of the average t reaches 9.09, the number published in 2018. Only the 2017 script, applied to TRPO, gets there.

Nothing proves this script produced the 2018 figure. Its name and its output make it likely. The t of 9 required no sorting: it comes from a test that counts episodes instead of random seeds. The flaw runs deeper than the figure said. The chance of the random seeds fooled the very test meant to measure it.

## the-best-runs | Keep the best, inflate by a third
Pineau showed another practice: run ten trainings and publish the mean of the best ones. Every number is true. The one published exceeds what the method gives on average. On our thirty PPO runs, keeping the best three out of ten inflates the mean by 33%: 2,249 instead of 1,692.

We applied the same selection to four algorithms on four tasks, ten random seeds each. The algorithms are PPO, written with two different computing toolkits (PyTorch and JAX), SAC and CrossQ, a descendant of SAC published in 2024. The tasks are HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker2d and Swimmer.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-meilleurs-sur-dix-en.svg | Horizontal bars grouped by task, four bars per task, one colour per algorithm, a dotted line at the median of +32%. HalfCheetah: SAC +19%, CrossQ +74%, PPO PyTorch +35%, PPO JAX +47%. Hopper: +78, +49, +29, +20. Walker2d: +63, +9, +58, +84. Swimmer: +8, +8, +18, +6. | Rise of the mean when only the best three of ten runs are published, for each algorithm and each task. All sixteen rises are positive: sorting always serves whoever does it.

The mean inflates by 32% at the median, from 6% to 84% depending on the algorithm and the task. Of 24 comparisons between two algorithms, 3 flip. Two of them pit the two versions of PPO against each other: when the gap is small, keeping one's best runs is enough to pick the winner. Redone on ten other random seeds, the same selection inflates the mean by 43% at the median and again reverses 3 comparisons out of 24.

In papers, the practice rarely shows: 14% of classical RL papers from 2026 let it show, against 19% in 2018. These shares are lower bounds, since a paper that keeps its best runs without saying so escapes our reading.

How many runs do papers launch? Of 41 experimental RL papers in the first 2026 draw, 20 do not say how many, 4 have only one and the median of the others is five, the most frequent value in Pineau's table. The number has not moved in eight years. Yet between two of our algorithms whose means differ by less than 20%, five random seeds recover the right order only 71 times in a hundred at the median. Measured on our twenty random seeds, this figure falls to 62 times out of a hundred. Such a gap reads backwards more than one time in three.

## the-leader-changes | The leader depends on the task, and on the budget
In 2018, Henderson and his co-authors showed that the leading algorithm on HalfCheetah stopped leading on Hopper or Swimmer. Our four algorithms of 2026 do the same. Each score is an interquartile mean: drop the lowest quarter and the highest quarter of the ten runs, then average the rest[[8]].

HalfCheetah: SAC 100, CrossQ 92, PPO PyTorch 27, PPO JAX 24. Hopper: PPO JAX 100, PPO PyTorch 94, CrossQ 75, SAC 43. Walker2d: CrossQ 100, PPO PyTorch 40, SAC 23, PPO JAX 23. PPO trains for a million steps, SAC and CrossQ for 200,000.

SAC leads on HalfCheetah, PPO written in JAX on Hopper and Swimmer, CrossQ on Walker2d. SAC, first on HalfCheetah, is clearly beaten on Hopper and on Walker2d. Yet the leader clearly beats all the others only on Walker2d: on the three other tasks, ten random seeds do not separate it from the runner-up.

One detail of the protocol weighs heavily: the budget, that is, the number of training steps. PPO trains for a million steps, five times more than SAC and CrossQ. So we read it at 200,000 steps, like them, under a rule written before looking at these scores. PPO then leads on no task. With ten random seeds, SAC still holds the lead on HalfCheetah. With twenty, CrossQ comes first on all four tasks, without clearly beating SAC on HalfCheetah. So the ranking depends on the task, but also on the budget each algorithm receives. A paper that compares two methods at different budgets compares those budgets too.

The two versions of PPO teach the same lesson. On Walker2d, PPO written in PyTorch scores 2,158, PPO written in JAX 1,272, or 41% less. The two intervals still overlap with ten random seeds. A method compared with "PPO" on this task would win or lose depending on the version chosen. With twenty random seeds, the rule fixed in advance names a winner on two tasks: PPO written in PyTorch wins on Walker2d, with 2,204 against 1,435. PPO written in JAX wins on Swimmer. The same algorithm, two libraries, two winners.

## modified-physics | Twenty percent heavier
Pineau asked that training be kept apart from evaluation. A student who saw the exam the night before passes without understanding anything. In RL, the agent is often evaluated in the very world it trained in. We evaluated our agents twice, with random seeds never seen: in the physics of their training, then with masses 20% heavier and friction 20% lower.

FIG std | rl-pineau-physique-modifiee-en.svg | Line chart on Hopper, from the training physics to the modified physics, one line per algorithm with its score and rank at both ends. Training physics: PPO JAX first with 3,064, PPO PyTorch second with 2,877, CrossQ third with 2,302, SAC fourth with 1,308. Modified physics: CrossQ first with 1,302, SAC second with 751, PPO PyTorch third with 609, PPO JAX last with 525. | Hopper, four algorithms, ten random seeds each. On the left, the score in the training physics; on the right, the score with heavier masses and lower friction. Ranks compare interquartile means, without a test.

Twelve algorithm-task pairs out of sixteen lose a detectable share of their score. The loss depends on the algorithm. On Hopper, PPO keeps only 22% of its score in its PyTorch version and 18% in its JAX version, while SAC keeps 64% and CrossQ 65%. PPO written in JAX, first in Hopper's training physics, ends last there. CrossQ takes the lead.

We had spotted this reversal after seeing the results, at a single strength. Twenty percent is only one choice among others. Before any new computation, we therefore wrote a rule: re-evaluate each agent changing one quantity at a time, masses from 30% lighter to 30% heavier, friction from 40% lower to 20% higher. A pair loses when the 95% interval of its score relative to the training physics stays below 1.

The loss grows with the change and draws a V. Masses 10% lighter cost 3 pairs out of 16 a detectable share of their score. 20% heavier, they make 12 lose. Friction matters less, except 20% higher: 9 pairs lose there. On Hopper, PPO written in JAX is no longer first at 10 strengths out of 12, with ten random seeds as with twenty. The reversal no longer depends on a strength chosen after the fact.

Have papers changed on this point? In classical RL, evaluation on held-out conditions goes from 33% to 45% of papers, with no detectable change. It has become the rule elsewhere, in RL for large language models, whose problem sets separate training and test by construction.

## a-baseline-too-good | A baseline too good to be true
The grid applies to ourselves first. One of our experiments was meant to illustrate a poorly tuned baseline. It sets PPO on Hopper with its library's default values against PPO with the settings published by the RL Baselines3 Zoo collection, the reference for this library[[9]]. First result: 2,877 for the default values, 1,003 for the tuned version. The published settings would do three times worse than the defaults?

The result was too good. Checking it, we found an error on our side: our code prepared the data the agent sees with a setting of 0.99, where the collection uses 0.999. Our error was real. It explained nothing: the corrected recipe does just as badly. Ten agents trained with the collection's official script score 995, against 2,423 for the default values. The published recipe has aged with the software around it. A result that looks too good gets checked to the end.

## coin-flip | A coin-flip reward
Today RL mostly serves to fine-tune language models, the programs that write text, like ChatGPT. They are given a math problem, a program checks the answer, the right ones are rewarded. In 2025, a team at the University of Washington showed that a reward drawn at random improves Qwen models in mathematics, but not Llama or OLMo models[[10]]. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B and the MATH-500 benchmark, the true reward gains 29.1 percentage points, a reward drawn at random 21.4. A coin that learns mathematics?

We wanted to see the phenomenon up close, at small scale. We wrote GRPO ourselves, one of the most widespread training algorithms for this use[[11]], then applied it to two small open models: Qwen2.5 with 0.5 billion parameters and Llama 3.2 with 1 billion. Each model trains for a hundred steps with the true reward, then with a coin-flip reward, three random seeds each time, then two more.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-grpo-graines-en.svg | Twenty runs, one arrow per run, from success at the start (a filled dot on the GPU, an empty square on the CPU) to success after 100 steps. Qwen with the true reward: 36%, 61%, 13%, 13% and 0%. Qwen with a coin flip: five arrows falling to 1% or 0%. Llama with the true reward: 20%, 48%, 25%, 27% and 33%. Llama with a coin flip: four arrows falling to 1% or less, a cross for the run stopped twice. | Share of problems solved at the first attempt, at the start and after a hundred training steps, for each model, each reward and each of the five random seeds. With the true reward, the same program gives very different results from one random seed to the next. With a coin flip, success falls to zero or nearly.

With a coin flip, the answer is plain: success falls to 1% or less in all nine finished runs. The tenth, a Llama run, stopped twice at its very first step, on a safeguard that halts training when its computations run away: we count it as a result.

With the true reward, the story comes in two acts. Over three random seeds, Qwen starts at about 10% and ends at 36%, 61% and 13%: the 2018 flaw in miniature. The mean gain is +26 points, with an interval from +2 to +51. With two more seeds, the new runs end at 13% and 0%. Over five random seeds, the mean gain drops to +14 points, with an interval from −3 to +34: it contains zero. Two runs were enough to undo the result. It rested on the first two, run on the GPU. The three runs that ran on the CPU, after GPU crashes, have a mean gain of −2 points. Hardware and random seed are mixed up there: five runs do not tell them apart. On Llama, the mean gain is +6 points, from just below zero to +15. Our part remains an illustration on small models, far from a replication of the Washington team.

## the-cutoff | The cutoff trap
This part brought a benchmark trap to light. The model's answers are cut at 160 tokens, the word pieces it produces one by one. With the base model, 23 answers out of 32 hit this limit before giving a result. None of these cut answers is right.

FIG std | rl-pineau-grpo-coupure-en.svg | Four rows of eight squares, one row per multiplication problem, one square per answer: green for complete and right, grey for complete and wrong, red for cut. 48 × 49: 1 right, 7 cut. 14 × 36: 2 right, 1 wrong, 5 cut. 21 × 50: 1 right, 2 wrong, 5 cut. 45 × 17: 1 right, 1 wrong, 6 cut. | The answers of the Qwen2.5 model with 0.5 billion parameters before any training, eight per problem. Most hit the 160-token limit before giving a result and count as wrong.

With the true reward, the model learns to finish before the cutoff: over the ten runs, the share of cut answers ends between 0% and 29%. With a coin flip, it ends above half in six runs out of nine, between 2% and 16% in the other three. Part of the measured "gain" therefore comes from a detail of the benchmark: learning to stop talking in time. We now measure the share of cut answers at every evaluation.

## what-we-keep | Listened to in part
Joelle Pineau was heard on what gets published alongside the result: the code, the compute, the effect of a setting. She was not heard on how to judge the result itself. The test remains rare, baseline settings remain poorly described, evaluation still often happens in the training world.

Our experiments sharpen the most famous flaw. Random seeds produce visible gaps. Our choices, sorting and keeping the best, turn these gaps into false positives. The t of 9 comes from another choice, a test that counts episodes. We draw three rules from this:

- never sort random seeds before comparing;
- show an interval next to every curve;
- test over random seeds, never over episodes.

Two traps lay in wait for us too: an error in our own code, which explained nothing, then a first indicator on language models that mostly measured a length limit. We found them because the protocol was written in advance and we checked surprising results before publishing them. That was Pineau's advice in 2018: be as critical of your good results as of your bad ones.

## seven-questions | Seven questions for reading an RL claim
The checklist is meant for authors. The reader of a paper, a press release or a demo can draw seven questions from it, which require no programming.

- **How many random seeds?** With fewer than five runs, or with "the best runs", the result says little.
- **What uncertainty?** A mean with no interval and no band around the curve does not allow a gap to be judged.
- **Which baseline, tuned how?** If the method got a month of tuning and the baseline its default values, the gap measures the month of tuning.
- **What budget?** Two methods trained on different numbers of steps also compare those numbers of steps.
- **Evaluated where?** An agent judged on the situations of its training may have learned them by heart.
- **What code?** Without published code, the reader does not know what the paper really did.
- **What compute?** A result whose compute nobody else pays for cannot be reproduced.

## the-full-document | The full document and the code
This article summarizes a much longer document, which goes through the sixteen flaws one by one, with all the numbers, their intervals and their limits: [the full document](https://github.com/warith-harchaoui/pineau-rl/blob/main/docs/conclusions-en.md). The code, the protocols written in advance, the training logs, the paper readings with their quotations and the scripts that regenerate every figure are in [the project repository](https://github.com/warith-harchaoui/pineau-rl)[[12]].

Our verdicts hold for classical RL, read by language-model agents on fifty papers on each side. A change of ten points still goes unnoticed there. Joelle Pineau asked that criticism apply to oneself first: ours is online, number by number, for anyone who wants to redo it.
