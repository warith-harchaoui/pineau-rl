BRIEF In short | Contents

Reinforcement learning (RL) teaches a machine to act by trial and error, guided by rewards and punishments. In 2018, Joelle Pineau showed two major conferences why the published results of this field inspired little trust. I found sixteen flaws in her two talks and took them up with two instruments. The first rereads the papers from 2018 to 2026 with her reading grid, the list of criteria she scored in each paper. The second reruns her experiments under a protocol, an experimental plan written in advance. My answer, after having eight years of papers read, rerunning her experiments and proposing new ones: Pineau was heard on what gets published alongside the result, much less on how to judge the result itself.

- **What changed.** In classical RL, the RL of robots and simulated games, with the same reading grid on both sides, linking the code went from 31% to 74% of papers, showing the effect of a setting (how the result moves when a setting changes) from 44% to 80%. Quantifying the compute, that is, how much computation the experiments needed, went from 27% to 58%, reporting an uncertainty from 63% to 88%. This last rise is the most fragile: it fails when I redo the analysis without the two papers read in part by another model, or with the "no" decisions judged doubtful turned into "yes".
- **What shows no detectable change.** The statistical test (6% then 12%), baselines (the methods a paper compares itself with) described with all their settings, evaluation on held-out conditions the program never saw during training.
- **The contribution of my experiments.** When the same program is launched several times, each complete training is a run. Two groups of these runs drawn at random rarely fool a statistical test. Two sorted groups, the best on one side and the worst on the other, fool it nearly a third of the time. Keeping the best three runs out of ten inflates the mean by a third. The t of 9 from 2018, the statistical test result that passed off two groups of the same program as two algorithms, probably comes from a test that counts the games the program plays during its training (the episodes) instead of the independent training runs, one per random seed.

ENDBRIEF

## three-rs | Three Rs, three definitions
**Reproducible.** A result is reproducible when someone else, with the same program, the same settings and the same task, launches their own training runs and reaches the same conclusion, such as "this program beats that one." The curves themselves move: chance during training changes them from one run to the next, from one computer to another too. The conclusion comes back when it was sound. An example from this article: I relaunched TRPO, the algorithm of the 2018 figure, thirty times, then applied the small calculation program that Peter Henderson, of Pineau's team, had published in 2017. The headline number of that figure, which I come back to below, came out of my own training runs.

**Reusable.** A piece of work is reusable when others take its parts (the code, the simulator, the published settings, the reading grid) to use elsewhere without rewriting everything. The parts still have to keep their promise in other hands, with other software around them. Here it is the parts that are judged, not a conclusion. An example from this article: the settings that the RL Baselines3 Zoo collection publishes for Hopper, the robot that hops on one leg. Reused with today's software, they give a robot that stands upright without moving forward. With the software of 2022, the same recipe made the robot run. A reusable part therefore travels with the list of its software and their versions: my repository pins the versions of everything it uses.

**Robust.** A conclusion is robust when it holds under a change it claims not to depend on: another task, another program for the same algorithm, a world slightly different from the one of training. This time, it is the conclusion that is judged. An example from this article: on Hopper, the version of PPO written with the JAX toolkit comes first in the physics it trained in. With heavier masses and weaker friction, it finishes last. Its lead belonged to that world.

Three questions, then. Reproducible: does someone else redo the same experiment and reach the same conclusion? Reusable: do the parts still do their job in other hands? Robust: does the conclusion survive a change it claims not to depend on?

Pineau chains the three words on one slide in Vancouver: "reusable material (software, datasets, experimental platforms) help us reproduce scientific methods to establish the robustness of the findings." Reusable parts make it possible to redo the work. Redoing it with another draw of chance, then under other conditions, shows what holds.

## two-curves | Two curves, one program
In the spring of 2018, in Vancouver, Joelle Pineau walks onto the stage of ICLR, one of the three major conferences in machine learning, the branch of AI where programs learn from data[[1]]. She is a professor at McGill University and runs Facebook's artificial intelligence research lab in Montreal. In December, in Montreal, she gives the talk again, in a longer version, at NeurIPS, the largest of the three[[2]].

VIDEO Vh4H0gOwdIg@1705 | Joelle Pineau, ICLR 2018: Reproducibility, Reusability, and Robustness in Deep Reinforcement Learning | The Vancouver talk, in full. The video starts at the moment of the two curves.

She shows two learning curves. A program, the agent, acts in a simulated world. At each step, it observes a situation, chooses an action and receives a numerical reward. It looks for the way of acting that pays the most in total. Here the world is called HalfCheetah: an articulated cheetah, drawn in two dimensions inside a physics simulator, whose six joints the agent drives. The agent scores points when the cheetah moves forward. At first it wriggles. After a million steps, it runs. Each curve follows the cheetah's score over training, averaged over five runs.

The two curves pull clearly apart. A statistical test, the calculation that judges whether a gap could come from chance, declares them different: t = −9.09, p = 0.0016. These two numbers, explained below, say that chance would be a very poor explanation for such a gap. A reviewer of scientific papers would see two algorithms, one better than the other.

But both curves come from the same program, the learning algorithm TRPO, with the same settings. Only the {{?random seed|The integer that initializes the pseudo-random number generator. It fixes the network's initial weights, the exploration actions and the simulator's initial states: the same code and the same random seed give the same draws.}} changes, the starting number from which the computer draws all the chance in a training run. Pineau says on stage that the two groups of five runs are not sorted: they are "just five runs" on each side. Chance alone produced a publishable "improvement".

FIG wide | rl-pineau-neurips2018-graines.webp | Slide "Let's look a little closer", subtitled "Both are same TRPO code with best hyperparameter configuration!". Two average-return curves on HalfCheetah, each the mean of five runs: the orange one ends clearly above the blue one. | Two groups of five training runs of the same TRPO program on HalfCheetah, differing only in their random seed.<br>Joelle Pineau, NeurIPS 2018, [page 22 of her slides](https://media.neurips.cc/Conferences/NIPS2018/Slides/jpineau-NeurIPS-dec18-fb.pdf), after Henderson and coauthors (2018).

Training draws many numbers at random, from the starting parameters of the neural network (the numbers that learning adjusts bit by bit) to the actions tried out to explore and the initial position of the cheetah.<br>Same program, same random seed: same result.<br>Same program, another random seed: another result.<br>A "run" here means a complete training with a given random seed.

The mean scores of two methods always differ a little, even when the two methods are identical. A statistical test says whether the observed gap exceeds what chance usually produces. To do so it computes a number, the t: the gap between the two means, divided by the variation chance usually produces. The larger the t, ignoring its sign, the sturdier the gap looks. From the t, the test derives the {{?p-value|Under the null hypothesis, that of no real difference, the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.}}, the probability of seeing a gap at least this large if the two methods were in fact equivalent. Below 0.05, one chance in twenty, researchers call the gap "significant" by convention. With two groups of five runs, a t of three already clears that threshold. A t of 9 is therefore enormous. It is the one on the 2018 slide, sign aside. A 95% interval gives the range of gaps compatible with the data. Built the same way on many samples, it would contain the true gap nineteen times out of twenty. If it contains zero, the gap may be nothing but chance.

The settings the experimenter fixes by hand, such as the learning rate or the size of the network, are called hyperparameters. To show that a new method does better, a paper compares it with existing methods, the baselines. The comparison is fair when all of them have received the same care in tuning.

The two-curve figure is taken from a paper by her team, signed by Peter Henderson and his coauthors[[3]]. It serves a broader point. Pineau starts from a survey by the journal *Nature*: of 1,576 researchers from every discipline, more than 70% said they had failed at least once to reproduce a colleague's experiment[[4]]. She then quotes two Stanford researchers, Buckheit and Donoho, who wrote in 1995 that a scientific paper about computation is merely the advertising of the research. The research itself is the complete software and the instructions that produced the figures[[5]]. Finally she shows, figure after figure, what her field lacks to deserve trust.

## sixteen-flaws | Sixteen flaws in four families
Pineau shows each flaw with an example. I found sixteen in her two talks, sorted into four families.

- **The material.** The paper comes out without its code, without the exact settings or the compute required, without the versions of the software used, on which the result also depends.
- **The tuning.** Hyperparameters are given in part, with no word on how they were chosen. Baselines get less care than the method being defended.
- **Random seeds and statistics.** Papers launch three or five runs, never ten in the table she shows. Only the best runs are published. Two groups of the same program look like two algorithms. No statistical test is applied.
- **The reach of the conclusions.** The best algorithm changes from one task to the next. Two programs coding the same algorithm give different results. The agent is evaluated in the very world it trained in, on benchmarks (the standard tasks everyone compares on) so simple it learns them by heart.

Eight years have passed. In 2019, NeurIPS adopted the checklist Pineau proposed, a set of questions every author fills in when submitting a paper[[6]]. Did we listen to her? Two instruments answer. One rereads papers with her reading grid, extended by a second grid for the flaws her grid did not measure. The other reruns her experiments with the algorithms of 2026, under a protocol written before the first training run: every later change is logged in it with its date and reason.

Most of my experiments use ten random seeds, hence ten runs, per program and per task. I later reran them with twenty. The protocol had fixed in advance that the first ten are authoritative: the numbers below come from those ten random seeds. I flag every place where the twenty qualify a result.

## same-grid | Reread 2018 before judging 2026
Pineau had read 50 RL papers from 2018 and scored eight criteria for each. I took every paper of the three major conferences, ICLR 2026, ICML 2026 and NeurIPS 2025, whose title mentions reinforcement learning, or 1,252 out of 17,836. A first random draw kept 50. Among them, 27 apply what they call RL to large language models, 14 belong to classical RL (robotics, games, machine control) and 9 are theoretical. Large language models are the generative AI models that write text, like ChatGPT. I drew more classical RL papers, up to 50, because fourteen are too few to detect a gap of thirty points.

Whom did I have read these papers? Generative AI programs, of the same family as ChatGPT. I salute these new readers, who even read the appendices. For each paper and each criterion, the reader decides yes or no, with an exact quotation from the paper and its page, which anyone can check in the project's online repository. I had ten papers reread by a second reader blind to the first reading. The two readers agree on 127 decisions out of 130 (98%). Since all these readers are copies of the same generative AI model, this agreement measures the model's consistency. No human redid the reading independently. A "no" could also hide a reader's oversight. With keywords fixed in advance, I searched for whatever could contradict each "no". Of 377 "no" decisions, 10 turn into "yes", 2 in 2018 and 8 in 2026. No verdict changes: my conclusions about the practices stay the same.

My rereading does not reproduce Pineau's counts. I had 50 papers from 2018 reread with her reading grid. This rereading finds 55% of papers giving all the settings of their method, whereas Pineau counted 90%. Comparing my 2026 sample with her numbers would mix two changes, that of practice and that of the reading. Each verdict therefore compares classical RL in 2026 with the 2018 papers as I had them read, with the same grids and readers of the same model.

Four practices changed, the four in green: the code, the effect of a setting, the compute and reporting an uncertainty (error bars or a band around the curve). Comparing eight practices gives chance eight opportunities to produce a false gap. I therefore tightened the threshold below which a gap counts, with the {{?Holm correction|The Holm and Bonferroni procedure. The p-values are sorted from smallest to largest, then each is compared with the threshold divided by the number of comparisons still left. It keeps the probability of at least one false positive across the whole family of comparisons below the threshold.}}, the usual rule when several comparisons are made. I also redid the comparison changing one choice at a time: without the papers read in part by another model, without each reader in turn, conference by conference, with each draw of the 2026 papers on its own or with the "no" decisions judged doubtful turned into "yes". Code and compute clear the threshold in all these variants, the effect of a setting in almost all. The rise in uncertainty is fragile: two of these variants make it fail, with p = 0.07 each time, above the threshold.

I also had the 98 experimental papers of both years reread by eight fresh readers, each of whom read papers from 2018 and from 2026: a reader's own strictness then weighs equally on both years and can no longer pass for a change between them. With their readings alone, linking the code goes from 31% to 72%, showing the effect of a setting from 42% to 78%, quantifying the compute from 31% to 66%: these three rises hold. Reporting an uncertainty goes from 69% to 86%, in the same direction, without clearing the threshold (p = 0.053). A reader from another model family recovers the direction of all four rises. When its "yes" decisions whose quotation cannot be found count as "no", the rise in uncertainty is no longer established (+7 points, from −9 to +23).

The other practices show no detectable change. The statistical test goes from 6% to 12%, baselines described with all their settings from 35% to 29%, evaluation on held-out conditions from 33% to 45%, the benchmark version from 23% to 26%. With 50 papers on each side, the survey spots, four times out of five, only a rise of 18 to 30 points, depending on the practice: "no detectable change" does not mean "no change". Nearly every paper now draws error bars, while very few apply a statistical test.

## a-slow-climb | A slow climb, with no jump after the checklist
Comparing two years does not say when, in between, the change happened. So I had 20 classical RL papers read per year from ICML and NeurIPS, in 2019, 2020, 2022 and 2024, drawn at random and read with both grids.

Code linked from 31% to 74%. Effect of a setting from 44% to 80%. Compute stated from 27% to 58%. Statistical test, in grey, from 6% to 12%, with 25% in 2024. | Share of classical RL papers following each practice, year by year: 48 papers from 2018 (those with experiments), 20 per year from 2019 to 2024, 50 from 2026. The four practices climb with no clear jump right after the 2019 checklist. The effect of a setting is an exception: it rises later, from 40% over 2019 and 2020 to 73% over 2022 and 2024. These series describe a slow rise that began before the checklist or with it. They do not say what caused it.

Every NeurIPS paper attaches the checklist filled in by its authors. Of the 30 NeurIPS 2025 papers my readers had covered, each had its answers compared with its text. The checklist's question on statistical significance (testing) settles for error bars "or other appropriate information". The 18 papers that answer yes to it apply no test. On code, 5 papers out of 22 that answer yes have no link to their code. A ticked box does not guarantee the practice it names.

The practices that progressed are not always the cheapest. Showing the effect of a setting takes extra training runs. The practice progressed despite that cost. Giving a benchmark's version takes one line of text, which is missing as often as before.

## thirty-seeds | Thirty random seeds, one program
Rereading the papers showed what changed in publications. My experiments now put the flaws to the test, starting with the most famous, the two curves from one program. I redid the 2018 experiment on a larger scale, with {{?PPO|Proximal Policy Optimization, by Schulman and coauthors (2017). A policy gradient algorithm that learns from the data it has just collected and bounds each update with a clipped probability ratio, so that the new policy stays close to the old one.}}, one of the most widely used reinforcement learning algorithms, in place of TRPO: thirty training runs on HalfCheetah, a million steps each, with the same code and the same hyperparameters. Between two training runs, the random seed differs and nothing else.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-e1-graines-en.svg | Thirty dots on a score axis, stacked where they crowd. Twenty-seven dots between 1,138 and 1,999, three red dots isolated beyond 3,000, up to 3,430. A dotted line marks the median, 1,580. | Thirty training runs of the same program on the same task, PPO on HalfCheetah. Each dot is a run, placed at its final score. Only the random seed changes from one dot to the next.<br>Mean 1,688, median 1,580.

The standard deviation, which says how far a run usually strays from the mean, is 568, or 34% of the mean. Not one line of the program changes from one run to the next. Even so, the scores do not draw a bell curve, the shape where they would bunch symmetrically around the mean: the three runs that take off pull the mean above the median, the score of the middle run.

Next, I drew two groups of equal size from these thirty runs, 10,000 times over. To each pair of groups I applied {{?Welch's test|The two-sample t-test that does not assume equal variances. The gap between the means is divided by the square root of the sum of the two groups' variances, each divided by its group size. Its degrees of freedom are estimated with the Welch and Satterthwaite formula.}}, the most common test for comparing two means. Both groups come from the same program: every gap declared significant is an error of the test, a false positive.

FIG std | rl-pineau-e1-faux-positifs-en.svg | Four grids of 100 squares, each square worth one hundredth of the draws. The first squares, framed with a dotted line, stand for the 5% threshold. Three against three at random: 1.6%. Five against five at random: 1.2%. Ten against ten at random: 1.7%. Five best against five worst out of ten runs, in red: 31%. | Share of the 10,000 draws where the test declares two groups of runs of the same program different, at the usual 5% threshold.<br>This threshold is the share of false positives a test used properly allows itself: groups drawn at random stay below it, sorted groups exceed it by far.

The result defies what the slide led us to expect. Two groups of five drawn at random almost never fool the test. The eye, though, is taken in. Their means differ by 17% at the median and by 47% in one pair out of twenty, enough to draw two well-separated curves. The test gives way once the runs are sorted, setting the five best runs against the five worst. Chance fools the eye, sorting fools the test.

## the-t-of-nine | The riddle of the t of 9
Where, then, does the t of 9 from 2018 come from, if groups drawn at random so rarely fool the test? Henderson and his coauthors did not test final scores. Their caption speaks of an average t over the whole learning curve: the test is rerun at each point of the curve, then the t values are averaged. I redid this computation on my thirty curves. In one draw out of twenty, this average t exceeds 1.4 when the groups are drawn at random, 2.5 when they are sorted. None of my 20,000 draws reaches 9.

First lead: with their algorithm, TRPO, scores may vary more from one random seed to the next than with PPO. I trained TRPO thirty times, with a plan written before any training. Its scores vary far more, since its standard deviation is 48% of its mean. Despite that spread, the average t exceeds 1.4 in only one random draw in twenty, 1.9 in sorted draws. The second lead is another way of computing t, which stacks the points of each group's five curves, a hundred in all, as if every point came from a separate run. In one draw out of twenty, its t climbs to 5.0 at random and 6.3 sorted. Worse, this computation declares a difference in 53% of the random draws, between two groups of the same program. None of these computations reaches 9.

The published number held the clue. A t of 9.09 with p = 0.0016 implies about 3.4 {{?degrees of freedom|The parameter of the Student distribution against which the test reads its t. The smaller it is, the more readily that distribution produces large values. For Welch's test, it depends on the variances and the group sizes and never falls below the size of the smaller group minus one.}}, a setting of the test that depends on how many measurements are compared. Yet comparing two groups of five runs always gives at least 4 degrees of freedom. At 3.4, this number cannot come from comparing five runs with five runs. It gives away a different computation. In June 2017, Peter Henderson had published a small program, a script, with a telling name: "from the same distribution"[[7]]. The script averages the curves of the five random seeds of each group. Then, at each point of the curve, it compares the two groups with a test built on how much scores vary between episodes, the successive games one agent plays during training. The variation between random seeds, the one the figure was meant to show, never enters the test.

### The eureka moment
I applied this script to my thirty TRPO runs. Here again, the plan came before the computation. The average t exceeds 9.09 in 38% of random draws. The test declares a difference in 43% of the cases. The 2018 number reappears in my own runs.

Recall that the t divides the gap between two groups by the variation the test measures. Measuring the variation between episodes is like comparing the average height of two classes while trusting the precision of the tape measure instead of the height differences between pupils. The smallest gap between the two classes then looks immense. The episodes of one agent play the part of the tape measure, the random seeds that of the pupils. In my TRPO, the standard deviation between episodes of the same agent is 7% of the standard deviation between agents, or between random seeds: divided by so small a variation, the same gap yields a huge t. With PPO, whose episodes vary more, the same computation never reaches 9.

PPO with my computation, TRPO with my computation, TRPO with stacked points, PPO with the 2017 script: no draw, an empty grid. | Two groups of five runs drawn at random from thirty, 10,000 times, for each algorithm and each way of computing t. Each grid gives the share of draws where the average t, sign set aside, reaches 9.09, the number published in 2018.<br>Only the 2017 script, applied to TRPO, gets there.

Nothing proves this script produced the 2018 figure. Its name and the numbers it outputs make it likely. The t of 9 needed no sorting: a test that counts episodes instead of random seeds is enough to produce it. The flaw runs deeper than the figure said. The test meant to measure the chance of the random seeds was fooled by it.

## the-best-runs | Keep the best, inflate by a third
Pineau showed another practice. Authors launch ten training runs and publish the mean of the best ones. Every published score was really obtained. Their mean still exceeds what the method usually gives. From my thirty PPO runs, I drew ten runs at random, a great many times. Averaged over these draws, keeping the best three out of ten inflates the mean by 33%: 2,249 instead of 1,692.

The same selection is then applied to four programs on four tasks, ten random seeds each. They are two versions of PPO, written with two different computing toolkits (PyTorch and JAX). Two other reinforcement learning algorithms join them, {{?SAC|Soft Actor-Critic, by Haarnoja and coauthors (2018). An off-policy algorithm: it keeps its transitions in a replay buffer and maximizes the reward plus the entropy of its policy, which pushes it to explore.}} and {{?CrossQ|By Bhatt and coauthors, presented at ICLR 2024. It removes the target networks and adds batch renormalization to the actor and the critic, to get more out of each interaction step.}}, a descendant of SAC published in 2024. The tasks are HalfCheetah and Hopper, plus two other simulated bodies: Walker2d walks on two legs and Swimmer swims.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-meilleurs-sur-dix-en.svg | Dot plot, one row per task, one dot per program, one color per program, a dotted line at the median of +32%. HalfCheetah: SAC +19%, CrossQ +74%, PPO PyTorch +35%, PPO JAX +47%. Hopper: +78, +49, +29, +20. Walker2d: +63, +9, +58, +84. Swimmer: +8, +8, +18, +6. | Rise of the mean when only the best three of ten runs are published, for each program and each task.<br>All sixteen rises are positive: sorting always serves whoever does it.

Four programs make six pairs, hence six comparisons on each task. Of 24 comparisons between two programs, 3 flip: the loser becomes the winner. Two of them pit the two versions of PPO against each other. When the gap is small, keeping the best runs is enough to change the winner. Redone on ten other random seeds, the same selection inflates the mean by 43% at the median and again reverses 3 comparisons out of 24.

In papers, the practice is rarely visible: 14% of classical RL papers from 2026 let it show, against 19% in 2018. These shares are lower bounds, since a paper that keeps its best runs without saying so escapes the reading.

How many runs do papers launch? Of 41 experimental RL papers in the first 2026 draw, 20 do not say how many, 4 have only one and the median of the others is five, the most frequent value in Pineau's table. The number has not moved in eight years. But between two of my programs whose means differ by less than 20%, five random seeds recover the right order only 71 times out of a hundred at the median. The right order here is the one given by the means over my ten random seeds. Measured on my twenty random seeds, this figure falls to 62 times out of a hundred. Such a gap reads backwards in more than one case out of three.

## the-leader-changes | The leading program depends on the task and on the budget
In 2018, Henderson and his coauthors showed that the leading algorithm on HalfCheetah stopped leading on Hopper or Swimmer. With my four programs of 2026, the leading program also changes from one task to the next. Each score is an {{?interquartile mean|The mean of the scores between the first and the third quartile, that is, the middle half of the runs. Less sensitive than a mean to extreme runs and steadier than a median when runs are few, it is recommended by Agarwal and coauthors.}}: drop the lowest quarter and the highest quarter of the ten runs, then average the rest, so that one outlying run cannot drag the score[[8]].

HalfCheetah: SAC 100, CrossQ 92, PPO PyTorch 27, PPO JAX 24. Hopper: PPO JAX 100, PPO PyTorch 94, CrossQ 75, SAC 43. Walker2d: CrossQ 100, PPO PyTorch 40, SAC 23, PPO JAX 23. Swimmer: PPO JAX 100, CrossQ 98, PPO PyTorch 96, SAC 42. | Score of each program relative to the best on its task, over ten random seeds: the bar at 100 marks the leader.<br>PPO trains for a million steps, SAC and CrossQ for 200,000.

Yet the leading program clearly beats all the others only on Walker2d: on the three other tasks, ten random seeds do not separate it from the runner-up.

One detail of the protocol weighs heavily: the budget, the number of training steps. PPO trains for a million steps, five times more than SAC and CrossQ. To compare on an equal budget, I also read PPO's score at 200,000 steps, under a rule written before looking at these scores. PPO then leads on no task. With ten random seeds, SAC still holds the lead on HalfCheetah. With twenty, CrossQ comes first on all four tasks, without its lead over SAC on HalfCheetah being established. The ranking depends on the task, but also on the budget each program receives.

The two versions of PPO, the same algorithm programmed with two toolkits, do not get the same score either. On Walker2d, PPO written in PyTorch scores 2,158, PPO written in JAX 1,272, or 41% less. The two intervals still overlap with ten random seeds: ten do not tell which version does better. A method whose score fell between the two would win or lose against "PPO" depending on the version chosen. With twenty random seeds, the protocol's decision rule names a winner on two tasks. PPO written in PyTorch wins on Walker2d, with 2,204 against 1,435. PPO written in JAX wins on Swimmer. After the Holm correction over the four tasks, only this second win remains established, by a small margin, 115 against 107.

## modified-physics | Twenty percent heavier
The ranking shifts with the task, with the budget and with the version of the program. Pineau also asked that training be kept apart from evaluation. A student who saw the exam the night before can pass without understanding anything. In RL, the agent is often evaluated in the world it trained in. It is that student, who may have learned this world by heart. I evaluated my agents twice, with random seeds never seen in training: in the physics of their training, then with masses 20% heavier and friction 20% lower.

FIG std | rl-pineau-physique-modifiee-en.svg | Line chart on Hopper, from the training physics to the modified physics, one line per program with its score and rank at both ends. Training physics: PPO JAX first with 3,064, PPO PyTorch second with 2,877, CrossQ third with 2,302, SAC fourth with 1,308. Modified physics: CrossQ first with 1,302, SAC second with 751, PPO PyTorch third with 609, PPO JAX last with 525. | Hopper, four programs, ten random seeds each. On the left, the score in the training physics. On the right, the score with heavier masses and lower friction.<br>Ranks compare interquartile means, without a test.

Take each program on each task: sixteen pairs in all. In the modified physics, twelve of them lose a detectable share of their score, that is, a drop too large to come from the chance of the random seeds alone. The loss depends on the program. On Hopper, PPO keeps only 22% of its score in its PyTorch version and 18% in its JAX version, while SAC keeps 64% and CrossQ 65%. These shares are computed run by run before being summarized with the same interquartile mean, which does not give exactly the ratio of the two scores in the figure. PPO written in JAX, first on Hopper in the training physics, ends last in the modified physics, where CrossQ takes the lead.

To check this reversal, I wrote a rule before any new computation: re-evaluate each agent, changing one quantity at a time, masses from 30% lighter to 30% heavier, friction from 40% lower to 20% higher. A pair loses when the {{?95% interval|Here a percentile bootstrap interval: the pair's runs are drawn with replacement, the interquartile mean of their score ratios is recomputed two thousand times, then the range holding the central ninety-five hundredths of these values is kept.}} of its score relative to the training physics stays below 1.

FIG std | rl-pineau-intensites-en.svg | Chart with two V-shaped lines, a dotted vertical line at the training physics. Masses: 8 pairs lose at −30%, 6 at −20%, 3 at −10%, 0 at 0%, 9 at +10%, 12 at +20%, 11 at +30%. Friction: 7 at −40%, 6 at −30%, 4 at −20%, 3 at −10%, 0 at 0%, 1 at +10%, 9 at +20%. | Four programs on four tasks, ten random seeds each, or 16 pairs of program and task. Each point counts the pairs that lose a detectable share of their score when a single quantity of the physics changes, masses or friction.<br>A pair loses when the 95% interval of its score relative to the training physics stays below 1. At 0%, the training physics serves as the reference: no loss is possible there.

The loss grows as the physics departs from training and draws a V, deeper for masses than for friction. On Hopper, PPO written in JAX is no longer first in 10 modified physics out of 12, with ten random seeds as with twenty. The twelve are the six mass settings and the six friction settings that differ from training. The reversal holds.

Have papers changed on this point? In classical RL, evaluation on held-out conditions goes from 33% to 45% of papers, a rise too small to tell apart from sampling chance. Held-out evaluation has become the rule elsewhere, in the papers that apply these methods to large language models, whose problem sets separate training and test from the start.

## a-baseline-too-good | A baseline a little too good to be true
Pineau's criteria apply to me first. One of my experiments was meant to illustrate a poorly tuned baseline. It sets two PPOs against each other on Hopper. The first keeps the default values of its software library, the ready-made software I take PPO from. The default values are the settings this library uses when nothing is tuned. The second takes the settings published by the RL Baselines3 Zoo collection, the reference for this library[[9]]. First result: 2,877 for the default values, 1,003 for the tuned version. The published settings would do nearly three times worse than the defaults?

The result was too good to believe without checking. I found an error of my own. My code prepared the data the agent sees with a setting of 0.99, whereas the collection uses 0.999. My error was real, but it explained nothing. With the error fixed, the published settings still lag far behind. Ten agents trained with the collection's official script score 995, against 2,423 for the default values, both groups trained this time on the benchmark version the collection was tuned for. A last run, planned in advance, found the setting that freezes the robot: how much the agent weighs the future. Set to 0.999 as in the collection, it makes every fall very costly: the robot prefers to stand upright without moving forward. Set to 0.99 in the learning itself and no longer only in the data preparation, it makes the robot run again: 2,649, as much as the default values. Yet in 2022 the same recipe ran with 0.999. The explanation I find most likely is that the published recipe has aged, because the software around it has changed since it came out. A result that looks too good gets checked to the end.

## coin-flip | A coin-flip reward
So far, all my experiments have taken place on simulated bodies, the kind on which robotics often works out its movements before trying them on real robots. RL's vocabulary has also reached the generative AI models that write text: some of RL's algorithms are borrowed (with a vague resemblance) to fine-tune their answers, with no agent acting in a world. They are given a math problem. A program checks the answer and rewards the right ones. In 2025, a team at the University of Washington showed, on three families of open models, that a reward drawn at random, without looking at whether the answer is right, improves Qwen models in mathematics, but not Llama or OLMo models[[10]]. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B and the MATH-500 benchmark, the true reward, the one that pays for right answers, gains 29.1 percentage points, a reward drawn at random 21.4. A coin that learns mathematics?

I wanted to see the phenomenon up close, on a small scale. I wrote {{?GRPO|Group Relative Policy Optimization, by Shao and coauthors (2024). For each problem, the model samples a group of answers. The advantage of each is its reward minus the group's mean, divided by the group's standard deviation, which removes the need for a critic network.}} myself, one of the most widespread training algorithms for this use[[11]]. I applied it to two small open models that anyone can download: Qwen2.5 with 0.5 billion parameters and Llama 3.2 with 1 billion. Each model trains for a hundred steps with the true reward, then with a coin-flip reward. A step here means one small correction of the model's parameters. I launched three random seeds each time, before adding two more.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-grpo-graines-en.svg | Twenty runs, one arrow per run, from success at the start (a filled dot on the graphics card, an empty square on the processor) to success after 100 steps. Qwen with the true reward: 36%, 61%, 13%, 13% and 0%. Qwen with a coin flip: five arrows falling to 1% or 0%. Llama with the true reward: 20%, 48%, 25%, 27% and 33%. Llama with a coin flip: four arrows falling to 1.25% at most, a cross for the run stopped twice. | Share of problems solved at the first attempt, at the start and after a hundred training steps, for each model, each reward and each of the five random seeds.<br>With the true reward, the same program gives very different results from one random seed to the next. With a coin flip, success falls to zero or close to it.

With a coin flip, the answer is plain: {{?success|Sampled pass@1: for each problem, the share of eight answers drawn at random that are right, averaged over the problems. An answer cut by the length limit counts as wrong.}} falls to 1.25% at most in all nine finished runs. The tenth, a Llama run, stopped twice at its first step, on a safeguard that halts training when its computations run away. I count it among the results, on the same footing as the other nine. In my runs, heads or tails, the coin always lands on the wrong side.

With the true reward, the story comes in two acts. Over three random seeds, Qwen starts at about 10% and one program gives three very different results: the 2018 random-seed flaw in miniature. The mean gain is +26 points, with an interval from +2 to +51.

With two more random seeds, the two new runs end at the very bottom. Over five random seeds, the mean gain drops to +14 points, with an interval from −3 to +34: it contains zero. Two runs undid the result.

The early gain rested on the first two runs, done on the graphics card. The three runs that ran on the processor, after crashes of the graphics card, have a mean gain of −2 points. In these runs, hardware and random seed are tangled together: with five runs, I cannot tell whether the gap comes from the machine or from the luck of the random seed. On Llama, the mean gain is +6 points, from just below zero to +15, while the accuracy of its most likely answer drops in all five runs, by 43 points on average. This part of my experiments remains an illustration on small models, far from a replication of the Washington team.

## the-cutoff | The cutoff trap
My GRPO training brought a benchmark trap to light. The model's answers are cut at 160 tokens, the word pieces it produces one by one. With the base model, before any training, 23 answers out of 32 (four multiplication problems, eight answers each) hit this limit before giving a result. None of these cut answers is right.

FIG std | rl-pineau-grpo-coupure-en.svg | Four rows of eight squares, one row per multiplication problem, one square per answer: green for complete and right, grey for complete and wrong, red for cut. 48 × 49: 1 right, 7 cut. 14 × 36: 2 right, 1 wrong, 5 cut. 21 × 50: 1 right, 2 wrong, 5 cut. 45 × 17: 1 right, 1 wrong, 6 cut. In large type beside them: 23 answers out of 32 cut. | The answers of the Qwen2.5 model with 0.5 billion parameters before any training, eight per problem.<br>Most hit the 160-token limit before giving a result and count as wrong.

With the true reward, the model learns to finish before the cutoff: over the ten runs, the share of cut answers ends between 0% and 29%. With a coin flip, it ends above half in six runs out of nine, between 2% and 16% in the other three. But a cut answer counts as wrong. A model that learns to answer more briefly gains points without calculating any better. With Qwen, the whole measured "gain" comes from this detail of the benchmark, learning to stop talking in time. Its cut answers fall from 75% to 6% while the accuracy of its finished answers does not rise. I now measure the share of cut answers at every evaluation.

## what-i-keep | Heard in part
Joelle Pineau was heard on the code, the compute and the effect of a setting, everything that gets published alongside the result. On how to judge that result, much less: the statistical test is still rare, baseline settings remain poorly described and evaluation often happens in the world of training.

My experiments pin down the most famous flaw. Random seeds produce visible gaps. Two choices by the experimenter make them worse. Sorting the runs turns these gaps into false positives, keeping the best inflates the mean. The t of 9 probably has another origin, a test that measured the variation between episodes and ignored the variation between random seeds. I draw three rules from this:

- never sort random seeds before comparing;
- show an interval next to every curve;
- apply the test to the gaps between random seeds, never to the gaps between episodes.

Two traps lay in wait for me too: a result too good to be true, behind which I found an error in my own code that explained nothing, then a first indicator on generative AI models that also measured a length limit. The protocol put me on the trail of the code error, while checking surprising results led me to the length limit. The protocol also keeps a dated record of every correction. Anyone can see what I changed and why. In 2018, Pineau advised being as critical of your good results as of your bad ones.

## seven-questions | Seven questions for reading an RL claim
The checklist is meant for authors. Anyone reading a paper, a press release or a demo can draw seven questions from it, none of which calls for any programming.

- **How many random seeds?** With fewer than five runs, or with "the best runs", the result says little.
- **What uncertainty?** A mean with no interval and no band around the curve does not allow a gap to be judged.
- **Which baseline, tuned how?** If the method got a month of tuning and the baseline its default values, the gap measures the month of tuning.
- **What budget?** When two methods did not get the same number of training steps, their gap also reflects that difference.
- **Evaluated where?** An agent judged on the situations of its training may have learned them by heart.
- **What code?** Without published code, nobody knows what the paper really did.
- **What compute?** A result that nobody else can afford to recompute cannot be reproduced.

## the-full-document | The full document and the code
I have been thinking about this almost since 2018. This article summarizes a much longer document, which goes through the sixteen flaws one by one, with all the numbers, their intervals and their limits: [the full document](/assets/pdf/reinforcement-learning-did-we-listen-to-joelle-pineau.pdf) (PDF). The code, the protocols, the training logs, the paper readings with their quotations and the scripts that regenerate every figure are in [the project repository](https://github.com/warith-harchaoui/pineau-rl)[[12]].

My verdicts hold for classical RL, read by generative AI programs on fifty papers on each side. At that sample size, a shift of twenty points can still escape measurement. Joelle Pineau asked researchers to criticize themselves first: my work is online, number by number, for anyone who wants to redo it.
