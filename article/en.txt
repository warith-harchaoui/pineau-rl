META Research | 2026-11-08 | November 8, 2026 | Same Code, Different Seed | Should I go to reinforcement learning? I never did: I had suspicions. In May 2018, in Vancouver, Joelle Pineau more than confirmed them. Here is what she showed that morning, what has changed since and my answer to whoever asks me the question in 2026.

BRIEF In short | Contents

Reinforcement learning (RL) trains a machine by trial and error, guided by a reward. In 2018, Joelle Pineau showed the ICLR conference that its published results did not survive a simple examination: the best algorithm changes from one task to the next; three implementations of the same algorithm give three results; two batches of five runs of the same program look like two different methods; papers publish three runs where ten are needed.

Eight years later, RL has earned its place: it fine-tunes ChatGPT and it taught DeepSeek-R1 to reason. The traps of 2018 came back with it, at a larger scale.

In 2026 we ticked again the grid she applied to the papers of 2018, on fifty recent papers drawn at random: held-out evaluation has progressed, baseline tuning has fallen back, statistical testing is still absent. My answer: three conditions, then a reading grid.

- **When RL is worth its cost.** An environment that runs for almost nothing, a reward you check without error, the budget for ten seeds and for a baseline tuned with the same care.
- **How to read an RL announcement.** How many seeds, which interval, which baseline, how many model families, which code.

ENDBRIEF

## why-i-never-went | Why I never went
I have been asked the question for ten years, in two forms. Students ask: should I go to RL? Executives ask: does RL really work? I have never done RL. I do applied mathematics with a computer, for companies, almost always supervised learning: you show the machine examples whose right answer you know, it learns to find that answer, then you judge it on examples it has never seen.

RL changes the contract. Nobody gives the right answer. An agent acts in an environment, a game, a simulated robot, a market; from time to time it receives a reward, a number; it adjusts its way of acting to receive more. The idea is superb. It beat the best backgammon players as early as the 1990s[[1]], then the best Go players in 2016[[2]]. My suspicions came from everything else. A single number, sometimes at the very end of a long sequence of actions, to learn everything from. Learning curves that zigzag. Spectacular results that nobody around me managed to redo.

I was not alone. In February 2018, Alex Irpan, an engineer at Google, published a post that stayed famous, "Deep Reinforcement Learning Doesn't Work Yet". He wrote: "Whenever someone asks me if reinforcement learning can solve their problem, I tell them it can't. I think this is right at least 70% of the time."[[3]] A shared suspicion is still a suspicion. I needed someone from the trade, who knew RL from the inside and who had measured.

## vancouver-may-3-2018 | Vancouver, May 3, 2018
On Thursday, May 3, 2018, at 9 a.m., Joelle Pineau opened the day at ICLR, one of the three major machine learning conferences, with an invited talk: "Reproducibility, Reusability, and Robustness in Deep Reinforcement Learning"[[4]]. She was a professor at McGill University and head of the Facebook AI Research lab in Montreal. She had been doing RL since 1999. At her first workshop on the subject, in 2000, there were thirty-five people in the room. In 2016, she counted about thirteen thousand RL papers published in the year. Nobody could accuse the speaker of holding a grudge against the field.

She started with a definition, from the US National Science Foundation: a result is reproducible when another researcher finds it again with the same materials; this is the minimum condition for a result to be believable and informative[[5]]. Then a number. In 2016, the journal *Nature* surveyed 1,576 researchers: 52% saw a "significant" reproducibility crisis, 38% a "slight" one; more than 70% had already failed to reproduce a colleague's experiment[[6]]. She had asked the same question to twenty machine learning experts at a workshop in December 2017: 80% saw a crisis.

Finally, a sentence from 1995, by two Stanford statisticians, Jonathan Buckheit and David Donoho, who took it from the geophysicist Jon Claerbout: "An article about computational science in a scientific publication is not the scholarship itself, it is merely advertising of the scholarship. The actual scholarship is the complete software development environment and the complete set of instructions which generated the figures."[[7]] The rest of the talk put that sentence to the test, on the ground she knew best.

## the-best-changes-with-the-task | The best algorithm changes with the task
The ground was policy gradients. A policy is the agent's rule of conduct: in this situation, take that action. You give it to a neural network and correct it little by little in the direction that increases the average reward. Four algorithms of this family served as baselines in almost every paper: TRPO, PPO, DDPG and ACKTR. She checked that the seven papers of this kind accepted at ICLR 2018 all compared themselves to them.

The test bench was MuJoCo, a physics simulator where jointed puppets learn to move forward: HalfCheetah, a two-legged half cheetah; Hopper, a hopping leg; Walker2d, a walker; Swimmer, a swimmer. The four tasks look alike: a few joints, continuous controls, unsurprising physics. You would expect the same winner everywhere. Her team ran the four algorithms on the four tasks, with five seeds each[[8]].

FIG wide | rl-classement-en.svg | Rank chart over four MuJoCo tasks. On HalfCheetah the order is DDPG, ACKTR, PPO, TRPO. On Hopper, Walker2d and Swimmer the order is reversed: TRPO, PPO, ACKTR, DDPG. On Swimmer the mean returns are 214, 107, 50 and 31. | The ranking flips as soon as you leave HalfCheetah. Mean return estimated by bootstrap, after Henderson et al. (2018), table 3. On HalfCheetah, the confidence intervals of DDPG and ACKTR overlap: even first place is fragile there.

DDPG dominates HalfCheetah; it comes last on the three other tasks. TRPO, last on HalfCheetah, wins everywhere else. On Swimmer, the HalfCheetah winner learns almost nothing. The whole ranking depends on the choice of task. An author who tests a method on HalfCheetah against TRPO and an author who tests it on Hopper against DDPG publish two easy victories, without anyone cheating.

## three-implementations | Three implementations, three results
She went further. Perhaps her team had coded these baselines badly? A search for "TRPO" on GitHub returns about ten public repositories, which she first found delightful: this is exactly the sharing she argues for. Her team's paper compares three of them, all serious: the original code of TRPO's author, the one in the OpenAI Baselines library and the one in the rllab library. Same algorithm, same task, same settings: three very different curves. The experiment repeated on three implementations of DDPG gives the same gap[[8]].

Then come the details that a paper almost never mentions. The size of the neural network changes the result. So does the activation function, the small computation each neuron applies to its input. The scale of the reward, between 0 and 1 or between 0 and 10, upsets learning, whereas with the methods that came before neural networks you could change it without consequence. These settings interact: the effect of the scale reverses depending on whether the network's layers are normalised.

Her next slide asked the awkward question: "When testing a baseline, how motivated are we to find the best hyper-parameters?" Every setting left untuned in a baseline becomes an apparent gain for the new method. Nobody needs to be dishonest. It is enough to tune your own algorithm for a month and the others for an afternoon.

## same-code-different-seed | Same code, different seed
Then came the slide I have never forgotten. Two learning curves on HalfCheetah. The orange one climbs towards 4,000; the blue one levels off around 2,700. Each is the average of five runs, with its uncertainty band. The two bands separate clearly. Any reviewer would see two algorithms, one better than the other. She let the suspense last, then gave the answer: both curves come from the same TRPO code, with the same settings, the best known. Only the random seed changes.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-neurips2018-graines.webp | Slide "Let's look a little closer", subtitled "Both are same TRPO code with best hyperparameter configuration!". Two mean-return curves on HalfCheetah, each the average of five runs: the orange one reaches about 4,000, the blue one about 2,700. | The slide as she reused it at NeurIPS in December 2018, where the answer is written on it. Joelle Pineau, NeurIPS 2018, [slide 22](https://media.neurips.cc/Conferences/NIPS2018/Slides/jpineau-NeurIPS-dec18-fb.pdf), after Henderson et al. (2018), figure 5.

The seed is the number that initialises a program's random generator. It decides the network's starting weights, the order of episodes, the small perturbations of the simulator. In supervised learning, changing the seed usually moves a score by little. In RL, early chance decides what the agent explores, what it explores decides what it learns; what it learns decides what it will explore next. A small difference at the start becomes a different behaviour at the end.

The published paper reports, for ten runs of the same program split into two groups of five, t = −9.09 and p = 0.0016[[8]]. Its caption states what this p measures: the average of the tests run all along the learning curve, not a comparison of final scores. And the paper does not say how the ten runs were split in two. With the usual 5% threshold, the gap passes for significant; yet it compares the same algorithm with itself. To understand how, I redid the experiment for real: thirty training runs of the same program, PPO on HalfCheetah, where only the seed changes.

FIG std | rl-trente-graines-en.svg | Dot plot in three columns. Worst 5 of a draw of 10 seeds: final returns between 1,138 and 1,524. Best 5 of the same draw: between 1,626 and 3,058. All 30 seeds: twenty-seven between 1,138 and 1,999, three above 3,000. | Thirty real training runs of the same program, where only the seed changes. On a draw of ten seeds, sorted and split in two, the best five score 42% more than the worst five: that is the median gap of this cut over a thousand draws, and the draw shown is the one closest to it. Together, the thirty form a single cloud.

The two batches of five do not come from two different distributions; they come from a single one, very spread out and not bell-shaped at all: twenty-seven of my thirty seeds finish between 1,138 and 1,999, three take off above 3,000. Drawn at random, two batches of five rarely fool the test: only 1.2% of 10,000 draws, for a 5% threshold. They fool the eye: the gap between the two means is 17% at the median and exceeds 47% one time in twenty, with no difference in algorithm at all. What fools the test is sorting: out of ten seeds, the best five against the worst five give p < 0.05 in 31% of cases. And keeping the best three out of ten inflates the mean by 33%. Add that a lab tries several seeds, several tasks and several settings before publishing: it always finds a combination that "works". That was my suspicion; the measurement sharpens it: the danger is not chance, it is what we keep of it.

## three-runs-never-ten | Three runs, never ten
How many runs do papers publish? She read the number in seven of the most cited RL papers of the time: the best five out of an unstated number, between three and nine, five, three, five, five, the best two or three[[8]]. None reached ten. She said so with sympathy, since a run lasts hours, even days, after weeks of tuning. Then she showed what this economy costs.

FIG std | rl-trois-sur-dix-en.svg | Three error bars. Baseline, ten runs: mean 41.7, worst to best from 36 to 47. My method, ten runs: mean 40.4, from 27 to 54. My method, best three runs: mean 50.7, from 47 to 54. | Over ten runs, the new method does not beat the baseline. Over its best three, it crushes it, with a spread three times smaller. After slide 30 in Vancouver; illustrative values.

Keeping the best three runs out of ten does two things at once. The mean goes up: that is a positive bias. The spread melts, since the bad cases were thrown away: the method looks more reliable than it is. A student who arrives with ten curves hears "your algorithm is learning"; with the best three, "we've got a trend, let's write the NIPS paper". She told the scene to make the room laugh. The room laughed, because it recognised itself.

She ended on the stakes. For twenty years, RL had only been used to play, backgammon then Go; its imprecision cost nothing. It was now arriving at medical treatment design, energy management, finance. Answering questions from the room, she listed her personal habits: ask as many questions of the good results as of the bad ones, document everything, rerun your experiments in the hours before submission. She also wished the major conferences would stop accepting papers without their code.

## what-she-obtained | What she obtained
She did not just talk. In the autumn of 2017, she had given her roughly 180 McGill students a final course project: pick a paper submitted to ICLR 2018 and try to reproduce its results. Other courses in several countries followed. The official tally, published later, counts 123 papers claimed by teams from 31 institutions[[9]]. Among participants who answered her survey, 54.5% reproduced part of the work and 32.7% most of it; 22% found the exercise very difficult. On the authors' side, 79% wanted their 2019 paper to go through the same examination.

In December 2018, in Montreal, she gave the talk again at NeurIPS with a new number. Her team had read fifty RL papers published in 2018 at NeurIPS, ICML and ICLR, then ticked a grid of eight boxes[[10]].

FIG wide | rl-grille-2018-en.svg | Bar chart in decreasing order: experiments 100%, neural networks 90%, method settings given 90%, baseline settings given 60%, code linked 55%, tuning method described 20%, evaluation on a held-out set 10%, significance test 5%. | Fifty RL papers from 2018, eight questions. The closer the box gets to proof, the less often it is ticked. Joelle Pineau, NeurIPS 2018, [slide 29](https://media.neurips.cc/Conferences/NIPS2018/Slides/jpineau-NeurIPS-dec18-fb.pdf).

Nine papers in ten give the settings of their method; six in ten those of their baselines. One in five says how its settings were chosen. One in ten evaluates the agent somewhere other than where it was trained. One in twenty applies a statistical test. The same talk asked a question I often quote: "Myth or fact? Reinforcement Learning is the only case of ML where it is acceptable to test on your training set." In supervised learning this would be a beginner's mistake. In the RL of 2018, it was the custom: you train the agent on HalfCheetah and publish its score on HalfCheetah.

She proposed a reproducibility checklist at the end of 2018. NeurIPS made it mandatory in 2019, at submission and again at camera-ready, together with a code policy and a reproducibility challenge. The report on this programme, published in 2021, measures the effect: fewer than 50% of accepted NeurIPS 2018 papers provided code in their final version; 74.4% did in 2019[[9]]. The checklist has since become the norm at the field's major conferences.

## measured-again-in-2026 | Measured again in 2026
A grid ticked in 2018 can be ticked again. We took her eight questions to fifty RL papers drawn at random, with a published seed, from the 1,252 papers whose title announces RL at the three most recent conferences: ICLR 2026, ICML 2026 and NeurIPS 2025. The readers are artificial intelligence agents (Claude, by Anthropic) that justify each answer with an exact quotation from the paper and its page; two independent readers, on ten papers read twice, agree on 127 decisions out of 130, or 98%.

The limits are four. Filtering by title leaves out RL papers that do not say so in their title. Only papers whose PDF is freely accessible enter the sample. Fifty papers give wide intervals. Joelle Pineau does not describe how she chose hers. To find out how our readers compare with her team, we first re-read twelve RL papers from 2018, drawn from ICML and NeurIPS, with the same grid: a difference counts only if it stands both against her numbers and against our re-reading of 2018. The protocol was written before the first paper was read; its deviations are recorded[[25]].

The 2026 sample contains 27 papers of RL applied to large language models, 14 of classic RL (control, games, robotics) and 9 of theory, 5 of them without any experiment. Percentages are computed over the 45 experimental papers.

Our re-reading of 2018 is the yardstick: it shows where our grid is stricter than Pineau's (method settings) and where it is more lenient (held-out evaluation).

**What improved: held-out evaluation.** Seven papers in ten now evaluate the agent somewhere other than on what it was trained on, against one in ten for Pineau and four in ten in our own re-reading of 2018, which is more lenient on this point. The progress comes mostly from RL on large language models: you train on one set of problems and judge on separate benchmarks, 26 papers out of 27. In classic RL, only 5 papers out of 13 do so. The "myth" of 2018, testing on the training set, survives where it was born.

**What regressed: baseline settings.** In 2018, six papers in ten gave all the settings of the methods they compare against. In 2026, fewer than two in ten, 18%, while our re-reading of 2018 still found six in eleven. Only one experimental paper in twenty, 5%, says it tuned its baselines with the same care as its own method. Her slide's question, "how motivated are we to tune a baseline?", now has a numerical answer.

**What did not move.** Linked code rises, from 55% to 67%, without our interval excluding the 2018 figure. One paper in four says how it chose its settings, against one in five. The significance test remains the exception: 4%, two papers out of forty-five.

**Ambiguous, and why.** Method settings go from 90% for Pineau to 67% for us; but our re-reading of 2018 gave 60%, because our grid requires every setting where hers probably accepted the essential ones. The gap measures our readers, not the field. The share of papers without experiments rises with theory; a sample of fifty does not allow us to say more.

The questions we added, taken from her Vancouver talk, say more. Of the 41 experimental RL papers, 20 do not say how many independent training runs support their main results; 4 rely on a single one; the others report five as the median. Uncertainty is shown by all 14 classic RL papers out of 14, but by 4 of the 27 papers on large models: the discipline Pineau asked for took hold in her own field, then got lost on the way to large models. Among those 27 papers, 16 try a single family of base models, exactly the trap that the random-reward study describes below. One in two, 47%, reports the compute used. Finally, best-run selection is still visible: an ICLR 2026 paper reports as its main result a score of 23.00, the best of three identical training runs scored 19.87, 21.58 and 23.00, without saying so. You have to read its hyperparameter sweep table to see it.

The data, the protocol and its deviations, and the paper-by-paper codings with their quotations will be published with the final version of this article.

## what-has-changed | What has changed since
Her warnings were confirmed by others, with means she did not have. In 2020, a team at MIT took PPO and TRPO apart: most of PPO's lead came from "code-level optimizations" that the original paper treated as secondary details[[11]]. In 2021, a team at Google trained more than 250,000 agents to measure the effect of more than fifty implementation choices of this kind[[12]]. The same year, Rishabh Agarwal and his co-authors showed that, with three to five runs, published rankings on Atari games often flipped when re-analysed with confidence intervals; they released a library, rliable, to do the computation properly. NeurIPS gave them an outstanding paper award[[13]].

Meanwhile, RL found the job it had been missing. In January 2022, OpenAI described InstructGPT: a language model fine-tuned by RL from human preferences, whose 1.3-billion-parameter version is preferred by raters to GPT-3 and its 175 billion[[14]]. On November 30, 2022, ChatGPT came out, built on the same method[[15]]. In March 2025, Andrew Barto and Richard Sutton received the Turing Award for laying the conceptual and algorithmic foundations of RL[[16]]. In April, Sutton and David Silver announced "the era of experience", in which agents would learn mostly from their own attempts[[17]].

The decisive turn came in January 2025. DeepSeek released R1, whose R1-Zero version learns to reason on mathematics problems by RL alone, without reasoning examples written by humans. The reward is verifiable: the final answer is right or wrong; a program checks it. The paper, published in *Nature* in September 2025 after peer review, reports a first-try success rate that rises from 15.6% to 77.9% on the AIME 2024 competition. It also gives the cost of the RL stage: $294,000 of compute, not counting the training of the starting model[[18]].

## the-same-traps | The same traps, only bigger
The RL of 2025 no longer starts from scratch. It fine-tunes a language model that already knows a great deal. Two 2025 studies then ask the 2018 question in a new form: what does it actually learn?

The first, by Yang Yue and co-authors at Tsinghua University, presented as an oral at NeurIPS 2025, compares a model before and after RL with verifiable rewards. It measures pass@k, the share of problems solved within at most k attempts. For a single attempt, the trained model wins clearly. When the base model is given hundreds of attempts, it solves as many problems, or more. The authors conclude that the reasoning found after RL was already in the base model: RL teaches the model to produce it on the first try, without adding new reasoning. Distillation, which makes a model imitate a stronger one, does add some[[19]].

The model after RL starts at 28% against 12% for the base model, but the base model's curve crosses it around 32 attempts and ends higher, 69% against 59%. | RL narrows what the model already knows: it wins on the first try; the base model catches up when given time. Shape after Yue et al. (2025); illustrative values.

The second, by Rulin Shao and co-authors at the University of Washington, is the closest to the Vancouver slide[[20]]. On the Qwen2.5-Math-7B model, RL with the true reward gains 29.1 points on the MATH-500 benchmark. With a reward that praises wrong answers, 24.1 points. With a reward drawn at random, with no relation to whether the answer is right, 21.4 points.

FIG std | rl-recompense-aleatoire-en.svg | Three bars of gain in points on MATH-500 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B: true reward 29.1, wrong answer rewarded 24.1, random reward 21.4. | On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, a random reward gives nearly three quarters of the gain of the true one. After Shao et al. (2025).

The explanation lies in a quirk of the training algorithm, which amplifies a behaviour already present in this model: writing its reasoning as code, without running it. This habit goes from 65% to more than 90% of answers; it happens to pay on this benchmark. The same treatment gives almost nothing, or lowers the score, on the Llama and OLMo families. The authors recommend validating any RL method on several model families rather than on the one model everybody uses. In 2018 the trap was called the seed; in 2025 it is also called the starting model.

The noise has not gone away either. A team led from the University of Tübingen re-ran in 2025 the evaluations of nine reasoning models with twenty seeds: on the AIME 2024 competition, the standard deviation of the first-try score ranges from 5 to 15 points from one seed to another; a single question more or less moves the score by 3.3 points out of thirty. Several gains announced for RL fell within that margin[[21]]. The cost has changed scale: a 2025 study on how to scale RL for large models used more than 400,000 GPU hours[[22]]. At that price, ten seeds become a budget line.

Andrej Karpathy, a co-founder of OpenAI, summed up the state of things in October 2025: "Reinforcement learning is terrible. It just so happens that everything that we had before it is much worse." He describes the agent that works a whole minute to receive a single grade at the end, then applied to every one of its actions, good and bad alike: "you're sucking supervision through a straw"[[23]].

Joelle Pineau went on to lead Meta's fundamental AI research. She left in the spring of 2025; in August, the Canadian company Cohere named her chief AI officer, based in Montreal[[24]]. She remains a professor at McGill.

## should-i-go-to-rl | Should I go to RL?
My answer has changed in form since 2018, not in substance. RL is an expensive, powerful, temperamental tool that pays under precise conditions. I check them in this order.

**An environment that costs almost nothing.** RL learns by trying millions of times. It needs a simulator, a game, an automatic checker, something you can restart without cost or danger. A real robot, a patient, a customer cannot be restarted a million times. Without a faithful simulator, RL remains an idea.

**A reward you check without error.** The success of 2025 comes from rewards that a program checks: the answer to the maths problem is right, the code passes its tests. As soon as the reward becomes an approximation, a grade given by another model, a business indicator you hope is linked to the goal, the agent learns to push the indicator up, which then drifts away from the goal. The random-reward experiment is a reminder that a measured gain does not prove the reward did the work.

**The budget for ten seeds and a well-tuned baseline.** If you cannot run each configuration ten times and compare against a baseline tuned with the same care and the same budget, you will not know whether you won. In that case, I start with the simplest method that works: supervised learning, distillation from a stronger model, better data. That is almost always where I end up.

When the three conditions are met, I go without second thoughts: that is what the teams now training models to reason or to code have done, with automatic checkers and compute budgets to match. When one is missing, I do not go. I then explain it to the client with the Vancouver slide.

### The grid for reading an RL announcement
When someone shows me an RL paper, press release or demo, I ask seven questions. None requires knowing how to program.

- **How many seeds?** Fewer than five, I learn nothing from it; "the best runs", I close the document.
- **Which interval?** A mean without spread says nothing; I want a confidence interval, or at least a band on the curve.
- **Which baseline, tuned how?** If the new method got a month of tuning and the baseline its default values, the gap measures the month.
- **On how many tasks, or model families?** One task, one starting model: the result holds for that pair.
- **Tested where?** On the training task, the agent may have learned by heart; I want an evaluation on situations it has not seen.
- **Which code?** Without published code, I do not know what the paper actually did; the decisive details are almost never in the text.
- **What cost?** The compute hours, in plain figures. A result you cannot afford cannot be reproduced.

## the-seed | The seed
I still have not gone to RL. What I did take up, that May morning in 2018, was asking every result the question she asked her students: did you question your good results as much as your bad ones? It applies to RL, to supervised learning, to my own curves. RL simply has the honesty to make the problem visible: its seeds produce gaps large enough that nobody can ignore them any more.

The two Vancouver curves had the same code. They did not have the same seed. The experimental science of this field plays out in what you do with that gap: publish it as a discovery, or measure it as noise.
