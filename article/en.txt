Eight years later, we reread the papers from 2018 to 2026 and reran her experiments with the algorithms of 2026. Four practices changed, others did not move. Her most famous number finds an explanation.

BRIEF In short | Contents

Reinforcement learning (RL) teaches a machine to act by trial and error, guided by a numerical reward. In 2018, Joelle Pineau showed two major conferences why the published results of this field inspired little trust. We took up her list of sixteen flaws with two instruments. The first rereads the papers from 2018 to 2026 with her reading grid, the list of criteria she scored in each paper. The second reruns her experiments under a protocol, an experimental plan written in advance. Our answer fits in one sentence: Pineau was heard on what gets published alongside the result, much less on how to judge the result itself.

- **What changed.** In classical RL, the RL of robots and simulated games, with the same reading grid on both sides, linking the code went from 31% to 74% of papers, quantifying the compute (how much computation the experiments needed) from 27% to 58%, showing the effect of a setting (how the result moves when a setting changes) from 44% to 80%, reporting an uncertainty from 63% to 88%. This last rise is the most fragile: it does not withstand all our checks.
- **What shows no detectable change.** The statistical test (6% then 12%), baselines (the methods a paper compares itself with) described with all their settings, evaluation on held-out conditions the agent never saw during training.
- **What our experiments add.** Launch the same program several times: each complete training is a run. Two groups of these runs drawn at random rarely fool a statistical test. Two sorted groups, the best on one side and the worst on the other, fool it nearly one time in three. Keeping the best three runs out of ten inflates the mean by a third. The t of 9 from 2018, the number that passed off two groups of the same program as two algorithms, most probably comes from a test that counts the games the program plays during its training (the episodes) instead of the independent training runs (the random seeds).

ENDBRIEF

## two-curves | Two curves, one program
In the spring of 2018, in Vancouver, Joelle Pineau walks onto the stage of ICLR, one of the three major conferences of machine learning[[1]]. She is a professor at McGill University and runs Facebook's artificial intelligence research lab in Montreal. In December, in Montreal, she takes up and extends her talk before NeurIPS, the largest of the three[[2]].

VIDEO Vh4H0gOwdIg | Joelle Pineau, ICLR 2018: Reproducibility, Reusability, and Robustness in Deep Reinforcement Learning | The Vancouver talk, in full.

She shows two learning curves. An articulated cheetah, drawn in two dimensions inside a physics simulator, learns to run. Each curve follows the cheetah's score over training, averaged over five runs. The two curves pull clearly apart. A statistical test, the calculation that says whether a gap exceeds what chance usually produces, declares them different: t = −9.09, p = 0.0016. These two numbers, explained in the next section, say that chance would be a very poor explanation for such a gap. Any reviewer of scientific papers would see two algorithms, one better than the other.

Yet both curves come from a single program, the learning algorithm TRPO, with the same settings. Only the random seed changes, the starting number from which the computer draws all the chance in a training run. Pineau says on stage that the two groups of five runs are not sorted: they are "just five runs" on each side. Chance alone produced a publishable "improvement".

The two-curve figure comes from a paper by her team, signed by Peter Henderson and his co-authors[[3]]. It serves a broader point. Pineau starts from a survey by the journal *Nature*: of 1,576 researchers from every discipline, more than 70% said they had already failed to reproduce a colleague's experiment[[4]]. She then quotes two Stanford researchers, Buckheit and Donoho, who wrote in 1995 that a scientific paper about computation is merely the advertising of the research. The research itself is the complete software and the instructions that produced the figures[[5]]. Then she shows, figure after figure, what her field lacks to deserve trust.

## the-cheetah-the-seed | The cheetah, the random seed and the test
A few words are enough to follow the rest. A program, the agent, acts in a simulated world. At each step, it observes a situation, chooses an action and receives a numerical reward. It looks for the way of acting that pays the most in total. The cheetah of the slide is called HalfCheetah: the agent drives its six joints and scores points when the cheetah moves forward. At first it wriggles, after a million steps it runs. Three other simulated bodies appear later: Hopper, a one-legged hopper, Walker2d, a two-legged walker, then Swimmer, a swimmer.

Training draws many numbers at random: the starting parameters of the neural network, the numbers that learning adjusts bit by bit, the actions tried out to explore, the initial position of the cheetah. Same program, same random seed: same result. Same program, another random seed: another result. A "run" here means a complete training with a given random seed.

The settings the experimenter fixes by hand, such as the learning rate or the size of the network, are called hyperparameters. To show that a new method does better, a paper compares it with existing methods, the baselines. The comparison is fair when all of them received the same care in tuning.

The mean scores of two methods always differ a little, even when the two methods are identical. A statistical test says whether the observed gap exceeds what chance usually produces. To do so it computes a number, the t: the larger the t, ignoring its sign, the sturdier the gap looks. From it comes the p-value, the probability of seeing a gap at least this large if the two methods were in truth equivalent. Below 0.05, one chance in twenty, researchers call the gap "significant" by convention. A 95% interval gives the range where the true size of the gap very probably lies: if it contains zero, the gap may be nothing but chance.

## sixteen-flaws | Sixteen flaws in four families
Pineau shows each flaw with an example. We found sixteen in her two talks, sorted into four families.

- **The material.** The paper comes out without its code, without the exact settings or the compute required, without the versions of the software used, on which the result also depends.
- **The tuning.** Hyperparameters are given in part, how they were chosen goes unsaid. Baselines get less care than the method being defended.
- **Random seeds and statistics.** Papers launch three or five runs, never ten in the table she shows. Only the best runs are published. Two groups of the same code look like two algorithms. No statistical test is applied.
- **The reach of the conclusions.** The best algorithm changes from one task to the next. Two programs coding the same algorithm give different results. The agent is evaluated in the very world it trained in, on benchmarks (the standard tasks everyone compares on) so simple it learns them by heart.

Eight years have passed. NeurIPS adopted the checklist Pineau proposed in 2019, a set of questions every author fills in when submitting a paper[[6]]. Did we listen to her? Two instruments answer. The first rereads papers with her reading grid, extended by a second grid for the flaws her grid did not measure. The second reruns her experiments with the algorithms of 2026, under a protocol written before the first training run: every later change is logged there with its date and reason.

Most of our experiments launch ten random seeds, hence ten runs, per algorithm and per task. We then reran them with twenty. The protocol had fixed in advance that the first ten are authoritative: the numbers below come from those ten seeds. We flag every place where the twenty seeds qualify a result.

## same-grid | Reread 2018 before judging 2026
Pineau had read 50 RL papers from 2018 and scored eight criteria for each. We took every paper of the three major conferences, ICLR 2026, ICML 2026 and NeurIPS 2025, whose title mentions reinforcement learning: 1,252 out of 17,836. A first random draw kept 50. Among them, 27 apply RL to large language models, 14 belong to classical RL (robotics, games, machine control) and 9 are theoretical. Large language models are the generative AI models that write text, like ChatGPT. Fourteen papers are too few to detect a gap of thirty points: we drew more classical RL papers, up to 50.

Who read these papers? Generative AI programs, of the same family as ChatGPT: we salute these new readers, who even read the appendices. They read every paper in full. For each paper and each criterion, the reader decides yes or no, with an exact quotation from the paper and its page, which anyone can check in the project's online repository. Ten papers were reread by a second reader blind to the first reading: the two readers agree on 127 decisions out of 130 (98%). All these readers are copies of the same generative AI model: this agreement measures its consistency, not a human's judgement. No human redid the reading independently. A "no" could also hide a reader's oversight. So, with keywords fixed in advance, we searched for whatever could contradict each "no". Of 377 "no" decisions, 10 turn into "yes", 2 in 2018 and 8 in 2026. No verdict changes, that is, none of our conclusions about the practices.

One gap separates us from Pineau herself. We reread 50 papers from 2018 with her grid. Our reading finds 55% of papers giving all the settings of their method, where Pineau counted 90%. Comparing our 2026 papers with her numbers would mix two changes, that of practice and that of the reading. Each verdict therefore compares classical RL in 2026 with the 2018 papers as we read them, with the same grids and the same readers.

Four practices changed. Linking the code went from 31% to 74% of papers, showing the effect of a setting from 44% to 80%, quantifying the compute from 27% to 58%, reporting an uncertainty (error bars or a band around the curve) from 63% to 88%. Comparing eight practices gives chance eight opportunities to produce a false gap, so we tightened the threshold below which a gap counts, as is customary in that case. We also redid the analysis several ways, changing one choice each time: these are our checks. Code and compute clear the threshold in every check, the effect of a setting in almost all. The rise in uncertainty is fragile: two of our checks make it fail, with p = 0.07 each time, above the threshold.

The other practices show no detectable change. The statistical test goes from 6% to 12%, baselines described with all their settings from 35% to 29%, evaluation on held-out conditions from 33% to 45%, the benchmark version from 23% to 26%. With 50 papers on each side, a change of ten points still goes unnoticed: "no detectable change" does not mean "no change". The contrast, though, is sharp: nearly every paper now draws error bars, very few apply a statistical test.

## a-slow-climb | A slow climb that began before the checklist
Comparing two years does not say when, in between, the change happened. So we read 20 classical RL papers per year from ICML and NeurIPS, in 2019, 2020, 2022 and 2024, drawn at random and read with both grids.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-trajectoire-en.svg | Five lines from 2018 to 2026, a dotted vertical line at 2019 for the NeurIPS checklist. Uncertainty from 63% to 88%, with 100% in 2024. Code linked from 31% to 74%. Effect of a setting from 44% to 80%. Compute stated from 27% to 58%. Statistical test, in grey, from 6% to 12%, with 25% in 2024. | Share of classical RL papers following each practice, year by year: 48 papers from 2018, 20 per year from 2019 to 2024, 50 from 2026. With 20 papers, a share carries an uncertainty of about twenty points either way. The four upper practices climb steadily. The statistical test, in grey, shows no trend.

The four practices climb steadily, with no clear jump right after the 2019 checklist. Only the effect of a setting is an exception: it rises later, from 40% over 2019 and 2020 to 73% over 2022 and 2024. These series describe a slow rise that began before the checklist or with it. They do not say what caused it.

The checklist itself had a surprise in store for us. Every NeurIPS paper attaches the checklist filled in by its authors. Of the 30 NeurIPS 2025 papers we had read, we compared these answers with the paper itself. The checklist's question on statistical significance, that is, on testing, settles for error bars "or other appropriate information". The 18 papers that answer yes to it apply no test. On code, 5 papers out of 22 answer yes without a link to their code. A ticked box does not guarantee the practice it names.

Another surprise: what moved is not always the easiest. Showing the effect of a setting takes extra training runs: the practice progressed all the same. Giving a benchmark's version takes a single line of text: that line is missing as often as before.

## thirty-seeds | Thirty random seeds, one program
Rereading the papers told us what changed in publications. Our experiments now put the flaws themselves to the test, starting with the most famous, the two curves from one program. We redid the 2018 experiment on a larger scale: thirty training runs of PPO, one of the most widely used reinforcement learning algorithms, on HalfCheetah, a million steps each, with the same code and the same hyperparameters. Only the random seed changes.

FIG wide | rl-pineau-e1-graines-en.svg | Thirty dots on a score axis, stacked where they crowd. Twenty-seven dots between 1,138 and 1,999, three red dots isolated beyond 3,000, up to 3,430. A dotted line marks the median, 1,580. | Thirty training runs of the same program on the same task, PPO on HalfCheetah. Each dot is a run, placed at its final score. Only the random seed changes from one dot to the next. Mean 1,688, median 1,580.

The mean is 1,688. The standard deviation, which says how far a run usually strays from the mean, is 568, or 34% of the mean. Twenty-seven runs end between 1,138 and 1,999, three take off beyond 3,000. Not one line of the program changes from one run to the next. Yet the scores look nothing like a bell curve, the shape where they would bunch symmetrically around the mean.

We then drew two groups of equal size from these thirty runs 10,000 times, then applied Welch's test to each pair of groups, the most common test for comparing two means. Both groups come from the same program: every gap declared significant is an error of the test, a false positive.

FIG std | rl-pineau-e1-faux-positifs-en.svg | Horizontal bars, a dotted line at 5%. Three against three at random: 1.6%. Five against five at random: 1.2%. Ten against ten at random: 1.7%. Five best against five worst out of ten runs, in red: 31%. | Share of the 10,000 draws where the test declares two groups of runs of the same program different, at the usual 5% threshold. Drawn at random, the groups fool the test less often than this threshold. Sorted, they fool it nearly one time in three.

The result defies what the slide led us to expect. Two groups of five drawn at random almost never fool the test, in 1.2% of the draws. They fool the eye, though. Their means differ by 17% at the median and by 47% one time in twenty, enough to draw two well-separated curves. The test gives way when we sort: setting the five best runs against the five worst gives 31% false positives. Chance fools the eye, sorting fools the test.

## the-t-of-nine | The riddle of the t of 9
Where, then, does the t of 9 from 2018 come from, if groups drawn at random so rarely fool the test? Henderson and his co-authors did not test final scores. Their caption speaks of an average t over the whole learning curve: the test is rerun at each point of the curve, then the t values are averaged. We redid this computation on our thirty curves. One time in twenty, this average t exceeds 1.4 when the groups are drawn at random, 2.5 when they are sorted. None of our 20,000 draws reaches 9.

First lead: with their algorithm, TRPO, scores may vary more from one random seed to the next than with PPO. We trained TRPO thirty times, with a plan written before any training. Its scores vary far more: its standard deviation is 48% of its mean. Yet the average t exceeds 1.4 only one time in twenty at random, 1.9 sorted. Second lead: another way of computing t stacks the points of each group's five curves, a hundred in all, as if every point came from a separate run. Its t climbs to 5.0 at random and 6.3 sorted. Worse, this computation declares a difference in 53% of the random draws, between two groups of the same code. None of these computations reaches 9.

The published number held its own clue. A t of 9.09 with p = 0.0016 implies about 3.4 degrees of freedom, a setting of the test that depends on how many measurements are compared. Yet comparing two groups of five runs always gives at least 4 degrees of freedom. At 3.4, this number cannot come from comparing five runs with five runs: it gives away a different computation. In June 2017, Peter Henderson had published a small program, a script, with a telling name: "from the same distribution"[[7]]. The script averages the curves of the five random seeds of each group. Then, at each point of the curve, it compares the two groups with a test built on how much scores vary between episodes, the successive games one agent plays during training. The variation between random seeds, the one the figure was meant to show, never enters the test.

We applied this script to our thirty TRPO runs, with a plan written before the computation. The average t exceeds 9.09 in 38% of random draws. The test declares a difference in 43% of the cases. A test divides the gap between two groups by the variation it measures. In our TRPO, the standard deviation between episodes of the same agent is 7% of the standard deviation between agents, that is, between random seeds: divided by so small a variation, the same gap yields a huge t. With PPO, whose episodes vary more, the same computation never reaches 9.

FIG std | rl-pineau-t-de-neuf-en.svg | Horizontal bars. TRPO with the 2017 script, in red: 38% of draws reach 9.09. PPO with our computation, TRPO with our computation, TRPO with stacked points, PPO with the 2017 script: 0%, a plain grey dot. | Two groups of five runs drawn at random from thirty, 10,000 times, for each algorithm and each way of computing t. The bar gives the share of draws where the absolute value of the average t reaches 9.09, the number published in 2018. Only the 2017 script, applied to TRPO, gets there.

Nothing proves this script produced the 2018 figure. Its name and the numbers it outputs make it likely. The t of 9 needed no sorting: a test that counts episodes instead of random seeds is enough to produce it. The flaw runs deeper than the figure said. The chance of the random seeds fooled the very test meant to measure it.

## the-best-runs | Keep the best, inflate by a third
Pineau showed another practice: run ten trainings and publish the mean of the best ones. Every published score was really obtained. Yet their mean exceeds what the method usually gives. From our thirty PPO runs, we drew ten runs at random, a great many times. Averaged over these draws, keeping the best three out of ten inflates the mean by 33%: 2,249 instead of 1,692.

We applied the same selection to four algorithms on four tasks, ten random seeds each. The algorithms are PPO, programmed with two different computing toolkits (PyTorch and JAX), SAC and CrossQ, a descendant of SAC published in 2024. The tasks are HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker2d and Swimmer.

HalfCheetah: SAC +19%, CrossQ +74%, PPO PyTorch +35%, PPO JAX +47%. Hopper: +78, +49, +29, +20. Walker2d: +63, +9, +58, +84. Swimmer: +8, +8, +18, +6. | Rise of the mean when only the best three of ten runs are published, for each algorithm and each task. All sixteen rises are positive: sorting always serves whoever does it.

The mean inflates by 32% at the median, from 6% to 84% depending on the algorithm and the task. Of 24 comparisons between two algorithms, 3 flip: the loser becomes the winner. Two of them pit the two versions of PPO against each other: when the gap is small, keeping one's best runs is enough to pick the winner. Redone on ten other random seeds, the same selection inflates the mean by 43% at the median and again reverses 3 comparisons out of 24.

In papers, the practice rarely shows: 14% of classical RL papers from 2026 let it show, against 19% in 2018. These shares are lower bounds, since a paper that keeps its best runs without saying so escapes our reading.

How many runs do papers launch? Of 41 experimental RL papers in the first 2026 draw, 20 do not say how many, 4 have only one and the median of the others is five, the most frequent value in Pineau's table. The number has not moved in eight years. Yet between two of our algorithms whose means differ by less than 20%, five random seeds recover the right order only 71 times in a hundred at the median. Measured on our twenty random seeds, this figure falls to 62 times out of a hundred. Such a gap reads backwards more than one time in three.

## the-leader-changes | The leading algorithm depends on the task and on the budget
In 2018, Henderson and his co-authors showed that the leading algorithm on HalfCheetah stopped leading on Hopper or Swimmer. With our four algorithms of 2026, the leading algorithm also changes from one task to the next. Each score is an interquartile mean: drop the lowest quarter and the highest quarter of the ten runs, then average the rest, so that no single outlying run drags the score by itself[[8]].

HalfCheetah: SAC 100, CrossQ 92, PPO PyTorch 27, PPO JAX 24. Hopper: PPO JAX 100, PPO PyTorch 94, CrossQ 75, SAC 43. Walker2d: CrossQ 100, PPO PyTorch 40, SAC 23, PPO JAX 23. Swimmer: PPO JAX 100, CrossQ 98, PPO PyTorch 96, SAC 42. | Score of each algorithm relative to the best on its task, over ten random seeds: the bar at 100 marks the leader. PPO trains for a million steps, SAC and CrossQ for 200,000.

SAC leads on HalfCheetah, PPO written in JAX on Hopper and Swimmer, CrossQ on Walker2d. SAC, first on HalfCheetah, is far behind on Hopper and on Walker2d. Yet the leading algorithm clearly beats all the others only on Walker2d: on the three other tasks, ten random seeds are not enough to separate it from the runner-up.

One detail of the protocol weighs heavily: the budget, that is, the number of training steps. PPO trains for a million steps, five times more than SAC and CrossQ. So we also read PPO's score at 200,000 steps, the same budget as theirs, under a rule written before looking at these scores. PPO then leads on no task. With ten random seeds, SAC still holds the lead on HalfCheetah. With twenty, CrossQ comes first on all four tasks, without clearly beating SAC on HalfCheetah. So the ranking depends on the task, but also on the budget each algorithm receives. A paper that compares two methods at different budgets compares those budgets too.

The two versions of PPO, the same algorithm programmed with two toolkits, do not get the same score either. On Walker2d, PPO written in PyTorch scores 2,158, PPO written in JAX 1,272, or 41% less. The two intervals still overlap with ten random seeds: ten seeds do not tell which version does better. A method whose score fell between the two would win or lose against "PPO" depending on the version chosen. With twenty random seeds, the decision rule fixed in advance names a winner on two tasks: PPO written in PyTorch wins on Walker2d, with 2,204 against 1,435. PPO written in JAX wins on Swimmer. The same algorithm, two toolkits, two winners.

## modified-physics | Twenty percent heavier
The ranking shifts with the task, with the budget and with the version of the program. Pineau also asked that training be kept apart from evaluation. A student who saw the exam the night before can pass without understanding anything. In RL, the agent is often evaluated in the very world it trained in: it is that student, who may pass by having learned this world by heart. We evaluated our agents twice, with random seeds never seen in training: in the physics of their training, then with masses 20% heavier and friction 20% lower.

FIG std | rl-pineau-physique-modifiee-en.svg | Line chart on Hopper, from the training physics to the modified physics, one line per algorithm with its score and rank at both ends. Modified physics: CrossQ first with 1,302, SAC second with 751, PPO PyTorch third with 609, PPO JAX last with 525. | Hopper, four algorithms, ten random seeds each. On the left, the score in the training physics. On the right, the score with heavier masses and lower friction. Ranks compare interquartile means, without a test.

Twelve algorithm-task pairs out of sixteen lose a detectable share of their score, that is, a drop the chance of the random seeds does not explain. The loss depends on the algorithm. On Hopper, PPO keeps only 22% of its score in its PyTorch version and 18% in its JAX version, while SAC keeps 64% and CrossQ 65%. PPO written in JAX, first on Hopper in the training physics, ends last in the modified physics. CrossQ takes the lead there.

We had spotted this reversal after seeing the results, at a single strength. A result spotted after the fact may hinge on a lucky choice: twenty percent is only one strength among others. Before any new computation, we therefore wrote a rule: re-evaluate each agent changing one quantity at a time, masses from 30% lighter to 30% heavier, friction from 40% lower to 20% higher. A pair loses when it does surely worse than in the training physics. In precise terms: the 95% interval of its score relative to the training physics stays below 1.

Friction matters less, except 20% higher: 9 pairs lose there. On Hopper, PPO written in JAX is no longer first at 10 strengths out of 12, with ten random seeds as with twenty. The reversal no longer depends on a strength chosen after the fact.

Have papers changed on this point? In classical RL, evaluation on held-out conditions goes from 33% to 45% of papers, with no detectable change. Held-out evaluation has become the rule elsewhere, in RL for large language models, whose problem sets separate training and test from the start.

## a-baseline-too-good | A baseline a little too good to be true
Pineau's criteria apply to ourselves first. One of our experiments was meant to illustrate a poorly tuned baseline. It sets two PPOs against each other on Hopper. The first keeps the default values of its software library, the ready-made software we take PPO from: the settings it uses when nothing is tuned. The second takes the settings published by the RL Baselines3 Zoo collection, the reference for this library[[9]]. First result: 2,877 for the default values, 1,003 for the tuned version. The published settings would do three times worse than the defaults?

The result was too good for our demonstration. Checking it, we found an error on our side: our code prepared the data the agent sees with a setting of 0.99, where the collection uses 0.999. Our error was real. It explained nothing: with our error fixed, the published settings do just as badly. Ten agents trained with the collection's official script score 995, against 2,423 for the default values. Our most likely explanation: this published recipe has aged, the software around it having changed since the recipe came out. A result that looks too good gets checked to the end.

## coin-flip | A coin-flip reward
All our experiments so far dealt with simulated bodies. Yet today RL mostly serves to fine-tune language models, the generative AI models that write text, like ChatGPT. They are given a math problem, a program checks the answer, the right ones are rewarded. In 2025, a team at the University of Washington showed that a reward drawn at random, without looking at whether the answer is right, improves Qwen models in mathematics, but not Llama or OLMo models[[10]]. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B and the MATH-500 benchmark, the true reward, the one that pays for right answers, gains 29.1 percentage points, a reward drawn at random 21.4. A coin that learns mathematics?

We wanted to see the phenomenon up close, at small scale. We wrote GRPO ourselves, one of the most widespread training algorithms for this use[[11]], then applied it to two small open models that anyone can download: Qwen2.5 with 0.5 billion parameters and Llama 3.2 with 1 billion. Each model trains for a hundred steps, each step nudging its parameters a little, with the true reward, then with a coin-flip reward. We launched three random seeds each time, then two more.

Qwen with the true reward: 36%, 61%, 13%, 13% and 0%. Qwen with a coin flip: five arrows falling to 1% or 0%. Llama with the true reward: 20%, 48%, 25%, 27% and 33%. With a coin flip, the answer is plain: success falls to 1% or less in all nine finished runs. The tenth, a Llama run, stopped twice at its very first step, on a safeguard that halts training when its computations run away: we count it as a result in its own right, not as a run to discard. Here, heads or tails, the coin always lands on the wrong side.

With the true reward, the story comes in two acts. Over three random seeds, Qwen starts at about 10% and ends at 36%, 61% and 13%: the 2018 random-seed flaw in miniature, one program giving three very different results. The mean gain is +26 points, with an interval from +2 to +51.

With two more seeds, the new runs end at 13% and 0%. Over five random seeds, the mean gain drops to +14 points, with an interval from −3 to +34: it contains zero. Two runs were enough to undo the result.

The early gain rested on the first two runs, done on the GPU. The three runs that ran on the CPU, after GPU crashes, have a mean gain of −2 points. Hardware and random seed are mixed up there: with five runs, we cannot tell whether the gap comes from the machine or from the luck of the seed. On Llama, the mean gain is +6 points, from just below zero to +15. This part of our experiments remains an illustration on small models, far from a replication of the Washington team.

## the-cutoff | The cutoff trap
Our GRPO training brought a benchmark trap to light. The model's answers are cut at 160 tokens, the word pieces it produces one by one. With the base model, that is, before any training, 23 answers out of 32 hit this limit before giving a result. None of these cut answers is right.

FIG std | rl-pineau-grpo-coupure-en.svg | Four rows of eight squares, one row per multiplication problem, one square per answer: green for complete and right, grey for complete and wrong, red for cut. 48 × 49: 1 right, 7 cut. 14 × 36: 2 right, 1 wrong, 5 cut. 21 × 50: 1 right, 2 wrong, 5 cut. 45 × 17: 1 right, 1 wrong, 6 cut. | The answers of the Qwen2.5 model with 0.5 billion parameters before any training, eight per problem. Most hit the 160-token limit before giving a result and count as wrong.

With the true reward, the model learns to finish before the cutoff: over the ten runs, the share of cut answers ends between 0% and 29%. With a coin flip, it ends above half in six runs out of nine, between 2% and 16% in the other three. Now a cut answer counts as wrong: a model that learns to answer more briefly gains points without calculating any better. Part of the measured "gain" may therefore come from a detail of the benchmark: learning to stop talking in time. We now measure the share of cut answers at every evaluation.

## what-we-keep | Listened to in part
Joelle Pineau was heard on what gets published alongside the result: the code, the compute, the effect of a setting. She was heard much less on how to judge the result itself. The test remains rare, baseline settings remain poorly described, evaluation still often happens in the training world.

Our experiments sharpen the most famous flaw. Random seeds produce visible gaps. Two choices by the experimenter make them worse: sorting the runs turns these gaps into false positives, keeping the best inflates the mean. The t of 9 most probably comes from something else: a test that counts episodes instead of random seeds. We draw three rules from this:

- never sort random seeds before comparing;
- show an interval next to every curve;
- run the test on the gaps between random seeds, never between episodes.

Two traps lay in wait for us too: a result too good to be true, where we found an error in our own code that explained nothing, then a first indicator on language models that also measured a length limit. The protocol written in advance put us on the trail of the code error, checking surprising results on the length limit. And the protocol keeps a dated record of every correction: anyone can see what we changed and why. That was Pineau's advice in 2018: be as critical of your good results as of your bad ones.

## seven-questions | Seven questions for reading an RL claim
The checklist is meant for authors. The reader of a paper, a press release or a demo can draw seven questions from it, which require no programming.

- **How many random seeds?** With fewer than five runs, or with "the best runs", the result says little.
- **What uncertainty?** A mean with no interval and no band around the curve does not allow a gap to be judged.
- **Which baseline, tuned how?** If the method got a month of tuning and the baseline its default values, the gap measures the month of tuning.
- **What budget?** Comparing two methods trained on different numbers of steps also compares those numbers of steps.
- **Evaluated where?** An agent judged on the situations of its training may have learned them by heart.
- **What code?** Without published code, the reader does not know what the paper really did.
- **What compute?** A result that nobody else can afford to recompute cannot be reproduced.
